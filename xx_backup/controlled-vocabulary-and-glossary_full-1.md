# Controlled vocabulary and glossary

This glossary focuses on physiological measurements and related methodological terminology used in thermal-environment research. It does not attempt to reproduce established built-environment terminology for environmental variables such as air temperature, operative temperature, or relative humidity, which are already well standardized elsewhere. Instead, it concentrates on terms that are used inconsistently across thermal physiology, environmental ergonomics, and measurement practice, especially where site, method, or derivation substantially affect interpretation.

## Thermoregulatory concepts

<details id="label-body-heat-balance" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Body heat balance</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The condition in which total heat gain equals total heat loss, such that no net body heat is stored.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>It provides the conceptual basis for interpreting whether the body is thermally stable, compensating, or accumulating heat.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>A state of body heat balance may be achieved with very different physiological costs, including sweating, vasodilation, or cardiovascular strain.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>When the term is used, it is helpful to clarify whether it refers to a theoretical balance condition, a model-based estimate, or an interpretation supported by physiological measurements.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>
<td>
<a href="#label-heat-balance-equation">Heat-balance equation</a>;

<a href="#label-thermal-load">Thermal load</a>; 
<a href="#label-thermal-strain">Thermal strain</a>
</td>
</tr>

<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Heat-balance equation</summary>

<tr>
<th>Unit</th>
<td>Commonly W or W·m<sup>−2</sup></td>
</tr>

<th>Definition</th>
<td>A formal representation of the balance between heat production, heat exchange, and heat storage in the body.</td>

<td>It connects environmental exposure, metabolism, and physiological response within a single interpretive framework.</td>
</tr>

<td>Different formulations emphasise different terms, but the central idea is that heat storage reflects the residual of gains and losses.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Interpretation benefits from specifying which terms are included, how they are estimated, and whether the equation is used conceptually or quantitatively.</td>

<details id="label-heat-storage" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Heat storage (S)</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Unit</th>
<td>Commonly W or W·m<sup>−2</sup></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The rate of change in body heat content resulting from an imbalance between heat production and heat loss.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>Thermal strain depends on whether heat is accumulating in the body, rather than solely on environmental exposure or perceived comfort.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Heat storage is a dynamic quantity; similar comfort states may correspond to substantially different levels of accumulated heat. In practice, it may be calculated directly from heat-balance terms or inferred indirectly through changes in core or mean body temperature.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Interpretation depends on how heat storage is derived, for example through direct heat-balance calculations or inferred from changes in core or mean body temperature.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>
<td>

<details id="label-thermal-load" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Thermal load</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The thermal challenge imposed on the organism by environmental conditions, activity, clothing, and other factors influencing heat exchange.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>The concept helps distinguish external or imposed demand from the physiological response to that demand.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Thermal load may arise from heat, cold, asymmetry, humidity, radiation, clothing, or activity, and does not by itself specify the magnitude of physiological strain.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Interpretation is strengthened when the source of load is stated explicitly, for example environmental, metabolic, or combined.</td>
</tr>

<details id="label-thermal-strain" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Thermal strain</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The physiological burden imposed on the organism by thermal load, expressed through changes in variables such as core temperature, cardiovascular activity, and sweating.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>Thermal-environment studies often prioritise perception, whereas thermal strain reflects the physiological cost associated with maintaining or failing to maintain thermal balance.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Thermal strain may increase even when subjective discomfort remains modest, particularly during prolonged or repeated exposures. The term therefore captures something different from sensation, preference, or comfort vote, even when these covary.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Operational definitions vary; interpretation should be tied to the specific physiological indicators used, for example core temperature, heart rate, sweat rate, or combined indices of heat storage and cardiovascular effort.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>
<td>
<a href="#label-heat-storage">Heat storage</a>; 
<a href="#label-thermal-load">Thermal load</a>; 
<a href="#label-body-heat-balance">Body heat balance</a>
</td>
</tr>
</table>
</details>

<details id="label-thermoneutral-zone" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Thermoneutral zone</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The range of environmental conditions within which body temperature can be maintained without substantial increases in metabolic heat production or active evaporative heat loss.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>It is a physiological construct and does not necessarily correspond to perceived comfort or preferred indoor environmental conditions.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Its boundaries depend on clothing, activity, acclimation, and biological factors such as age and sex. In interdisciplinary contexts, the term is often conflated with comfort zone or acceptable indoor range, although these refer to different criteria.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>The term is most useful when referring explicitly to physiological regulation rather than to acceptable or desirable indoor conditions.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>
<td>
<a href="#label-thermoregulatory-set-point">Thermoregulatory set-point</a>; 
<a href="#label-interthreshold-zone">Interthreshold zone</a>; 
<a href="#label-thermal-strain">Thermal strain</a>
</td>
</tr>

<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Thermoregulatory set-point</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The defended value of a regulated thermal variable around which thermoeffector responses are organised.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>It helps explain why similar body temperatures may correspond to different regulatory states, such as fever, hormonal modulation, or normal thermoregulation.</td>
</tr>

<th>Notes</th>
<td>In biological systems, set-points are better understood as functional regulatory references than as fixed universal temperatures.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Interpretation benefits from stating whether the term is used conceptually, inferred from threshold shifts, or tied to a specific regulatory model.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>

<details id="label-interthreshold-zone" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Interthreshold zone</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The range of body temperatures bounded by the activation thresholds for heat-loss and heat-gain effectors, within which overt thermoeffector responses are minimal.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>It provides a mechanistic way to think about when sweating, vasodilation, vasoconstriction, or shivering begin, and why those thresholds vary across physiological states.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Its width and position can shift with circadian timing, sex hormones, fever, acclimation, or ageing.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>When invoking threshold shifts, it is helpful to specify the effector concerned and the variable used to define onset.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>
<td>
<a href="#label-thermoregulatory-set-point">Thermoregulatory set-point</a>; 
<a href="#label-effector-activation-thresholds">Effector activation thresholds</a>; 
<a href="#label-thermoneutral-zone">Thermoneutral zone</a>
</td>

<table class="glossary-spec">

<th>Definition</th>
<td>Changes that reduce the physiological strain produced by environmental stressors; these changes may be phenotypic or genotypic.</td>
</tr>

<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>The term is broader than most experimental studies intend, and unqualified use can obscure whether changes are short-term or long-term.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>In thermal physiology, adaptation is often used for reduced strain under repeated or chronic exposure, but its exact meaning benefits from further specification.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>It is useful to specify whether the term refers to phenotypic, genotypic, seasonal, or longer-term processes.</td>
</tr>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>Physiological or behavioural changes occurring within an organism in response to experimentally induced changes in specific climatic factors.</td>
</tr>

<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>The term distinguishes responses induced under controlled experimental conditions from responses arising from seasonal or natural environmental exposure.</td>

<td>In thermal physiology, acclimation is typically treated as a form of phenotypic adaptation to a defined environmental stimulus.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>The term is most precise when the exposure is experimentally imposed and clearly described.</td>
</tr>

## Physiological control systems

<details id="label-thermoregulation" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Thermoregulation</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The coordinated physiological and behavioural processes by which the body maintains internal thermal stability in changing conditions.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>It is the overarching regulatory system linking central control, peripheral effectors, and environmental exchange.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Thermoregulation includes both autonomic and behavioural components and operates across multiple timescales, from rapid vasomotor responses to longer-term acclimation.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Use of the term is strengthened when the relevant regulatory level—central, peripheral, autonomic, or behavioural—is made explicit.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>
<td>
<a href="#label-hypothalamic-thermoregulatory-circuits">Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits</a>; 
<a href="#label-thermoeffector">Thermoeffector</a>;

<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>Neural control pathways centred in the hypothalamus that integrate thermal information and coordinate thermoeffector responses.</td>

<td>They provide a mechanistic basis for understanding how body and environmental thermal signals are translated into physiological action.</td>

<td>These circuits are commonly discussed as central regulators of vasomotion, sweating, shivering, and behavioural responses, although thermoregulation is distributed across broader neural networks.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>The term is most useful when referring to central integration rather than to peripheral effectors themselves.</td>
</tr>
<tr>

<details id="label-feedback-and-feed-forward-control" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Feedback and feed-forward control</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>Modes of physiological regulation in which responses are driven either by deviations from a regulated state (feedback) or by anticipatory or predictive cues (feed-forward).</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>The distinction helps explain why thermoregulatory responses may begin before large internal temperature changes occur or continue to reflect internal state after environmental perturbation.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Thermoregulation involves both forms of control, and many observed responses represent their interaction rather than a purely reactive mechanism.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Interpretation is improved when authors distinguish between responses triggered by measured internal state and those triggered by anticipation, context, or rapidly changing external conditions.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>
<td>
<a href="#label-thermoregulation">Thermoregulation</a>;

<td>A family of methods used to estimate energy expenditure or heat exchange, either by measuring heat directly or by inferring it from gas exchange.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>It provides the methodological bridge between metabolism and thermal interpretation.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Direct calorimetry refers to direct measurement of heat transfer, whereas indirect calorimetry estimates energy expenditure from oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Interpretation should state whether calorimetry is direct or indirect and which derived variables are used in subsequent analysis.</td>
</tr>

<details id="label-distal-proximal-gradient" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Distal–proximal gradient (DPG)</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Unit</th>
<td>°C</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>The temperature difference between distal skin regions, such as the hands or feet, and more proximal regions, such as the torso or thigh.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>The gradient provides a sensitive indicator of peripheral vasomotor state and is widely used in studies of thermoregulation, circadian physiology, and sleep onset.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>More positive values are generally associated with distal warming and vasodilation, whereas lower values reflect vasoconstriction. Interpretation depends on the exact sites included in each compartment.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Interpretation depends on the anatomical sites included, the averaging procedures applied, and the sign convention used.</td>
</tr>

## Anatomical reference and spatial conventions

<details id="label-spatial-orientation" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Spatial orientation (anterior, posterior, medial, lateral, palmar, dorsal)</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>Descriptors used to define the position of anatomical sites relative to standard body axes and surfaces.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Sub-types</th>
<td>
<b>Anterior</b>: front side of the body<br>
<b>Posterior</b>: back side of the body<br>
<b>Medial</b>: closer to the midline<br>
<b>Lateral</b>: further from the midline<br>
<b>Palmar</b>: anterior surface of the hand (glabrous skin)<br>
<b>Dorsal</b>: posterior surface of the hand or foot (non-glabrous skin)
</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>Clear orientation terminology is essential for reproducible description of measurement sites, especially when comparing studies or mapping physiological responses across the body.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Palmar and dorsal are region-specific descriptors and should be used consistently when referring to hands and feet. In interdisciplinary contexts, terms such as “front” or “back” should be avoided in favour of standard anatomical terminology.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>Measurement sites should be described using standard orientation terms rather than informal descriptors, particularly when spatial gradients or asymmetries are analysed.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Related concepts</th>
<td>
<a href="#label-longitudinal-position">Longitudinal position</a>;

<details id="label-longitudinal-position" class="glossary-entry">
<summary class="glossary-entry-title">Longitudinal position (proximal, distal)</summary>

<table class="glossary-spec">
<tr>
<th>Definition</th>
<td>Descriptors used to define the position of a site along the length of a limb or anatomical structure relative to its point of origin.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Sub-types</th>
<td>
<b>Proximal</b>: closer to the trunk or point of origin<br>
<b>Distal</b>: further from the trunk or point of origin
</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Why it matters</th>
<td>Proximal–distal distinctions are central to interpreting thermal gradients, vascular responses, and measurement-site differences, particularly in limbs.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Notes</th>
<td>Distal regions (e.g. hands and feet) often exhibit stronger vasomotor responses and greater temperature variability than proximal regions.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th>Reporting recommendation</th>
<td>When describing measurement locations, proximal or distal qualifiers should be included where relevant, especially in studies involving limbs or gradients such as distal–proximal temperature differences.</td>
</tr>
