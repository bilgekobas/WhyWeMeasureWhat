# CONFOUNDER × SIGNAL: SYNTHESIS OF MODIFIERS




The “Known confounders and modifiers” subsections in Chapter <a class="citation" href="/09_references/09_01_references/#ref-3" data-cite="3">[3]</a>(#physiological-signals) describe, for each signal, how participant characteristics, timing, protocol choices, and underlying conditions shape recorded values and responses. Table 12 summarises these influences across signals. Rows correspond to the main modifier headings used in Chapter <a class="citation" href="/09_references/09_01_references/#ref-3" data-cite="3">[3]</a>(#physiological-signals), and columns group related physiological indicators: core body temperature (CBT), mean and regional skin temperature (Tsk, distal–proximal gradient, DPG), heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), blood pressure (BP), sweating and whole-body sweat loss (Sweat / WBSL), and electrodermal activity (EDA). Within each cell, short phrases describe the typical direction or nature of the effect relative to a young, healthy, well-rested adult under thermoneutral indoor conditions (e.g. “↑ baseline”, “↓ gain”, “phase delay”, “blunted amplitude”). The table is intended as a planning and interpretation aid; fuller mechanisms, exceptions, and references are given in the respective signal-specific sections of Chapter <a class="citation" href="/09_references/09_01_references/#ref-3" data-cite="3">[3]</a>(#physiological-signals).



| **Modifier** | **CBT** | **Tsk / DPG** | **HR** | **HRV** | **BP** | **Sweat / WBSL** | **EDA** |

|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|

| **Circadian rhythm and behavioural influences** | Strong 24-h rhythm (nocturnal nadir, late-afternoon peak); masking by sleep, posture, activity and light can shift observed CBT. | Distal sites warm in evening and biological night; proximal Tsk mirrors CBT; DPG increases before sleep onset; posture, activity, light/screens can blunt or accentuate these rhythms. | Higher by day, lower at night; morning rise after waking; recent activity and posture changes add short-term variability. | Vagal indices (RMSSD, HF) highest at night, reduced by day; behavioural masking and irregular schedules flatten amplitude. | Clear day–night pattern with “dipping” at night and morning surge; irregular schedules and pre-measurement behaviour (talking, walking, screens) distort profiles. | Diurnal pattern in sweat onset and gain; responses weakest in morning and strongest late afternoon/early evening; behaviours that modify thermal load or sympathetic tone modulate this. | Tonic level and SCR frequency often higher in afternoon/evening, lower at night; behavioural state (task engagement, posture, sensory load) strongly shapes moment-to-moment variation. |

| **Chronotype, sleep timing, and misalignment** | Morning types: advanced phase and earlier CBT peak; evening types: delayed peak, often slightly higher nocturnal CBT; short/irregular sleep lowers amplitude and delays phase. | Evening types show delayed distal warming and reduced nocturnal DPG amplitude; sleep restriction/misalignment delays distal vasodilation and blunts night-time rhythm. | Irregular or restricted sleep elevates nocturnal HR and reduces day–night contrast. | Reduced nocturnal RMSSD/HF and flatter 24-h profile with sleep restriction and circadian misalignment. | Shift work, social jetlag, and irregular sleep–wake timing increase non-dipping and reverse-dipping patterns. | Misalignment can delay sweat onset and reduce diurnal variation in sudomotor responses. | Sleep restriction and misalignment dampen nocturnal SCL minima and SCR rhythms; EDA rhythm may drift away from CBT phase. |

| **Sex hormones, reproductive status, contraceptives, menopause, and exogenous hormones (OCP, HRT, GAHT)** | Luteal phase / progestin-dominant states ↑ resting CBT by ~0.3–0.5 °C and narrow thermoneutral zone; oestrogen lowers defended CBT; combined OCP produces chronically higher, flatter CBT; peri-/postmenopausal instability and HRT/GAHT shift defended level towards current hormonal profile. | Luteal and progestin-dominant states ↑ baseline Tsk and delay vasodilation; oestrogen enhances skin perfusion and stabilises Tsk rhythms; combined OCP: higher Tsk but blunted cycling; menopause and some HRT regimens cause regional vasomotor instability (flushes). | Ovarian hormone fluctuations and exogenous regimens produce small phase-dependent HR shifts; androgens (including masculinising GAHT) tend to ↑ HR over time. | Oestrogen-dominant states and some HRT regimens ↑ vagal HRV; luteal/low-oestrogen states and some OCP/GAHT protocols ↓ HRV and dampen cyclicity. | Premenopausal women have lower BP than men; combined OCP and some HRT/GAHT ↑ BP and hypertension risk; postmenopausal trajectories show steeper SBP and pulse-pressure rise. | Progesterone raises, and oestrogen lowers, sweating thresholds; OCP and HRT shift sudomotor thresholds accordingly; testosterone/androgen-dominant states increase sweating capacity; patterns under GAHT generally mirror affirmed hormonal profile. | Vasomotor events (flushes) appear as transient EDA surges; hormonal state shapes tonic levels, circadian amplitude, and magnitude of thermal and non-thermal EDA responses. |

| **Age** | Similar mean CBT but reduced circadian amplitude and slower dynamics; older adults exhibit larger CBT excursions under heat/cold when effectors are limited. | Reduced maximal vasodilation, cooler and more slowly warming extremities; more heterogeneous regional Tsk; altered DPG patterns. | Lower maximal HR, often slightly higher resting HR; slower on- and off-responses. | Progressive decline in vagal HRV and baroreflex sensitivity; less flexible autonomic modulation. | Higher SBP and pulse pressure, increased BP variability; greater seasonal and thermal sensitivity; more orthostatic instability. | Declining gland output and sensitivity; slower onset, lower maximal sweat rate, less uniform regional sweating. | Lower tonic SCL and smaller SCR amplitudes; reduced sympathetic-skin reactivity and slower recovery. |

| **Fitness and heat acclimation** | Aerobically trained and heat-acclimated individuals show slightly lower resting CBT and smaller CBT excursions for a given heat load; faster recovery after exposure. | Earlier and larger cutaneous vasodilatory responses; higher distal Tsk and more stable gradients at a given load; acclimation improves shell-core coordination. | Fitness and acclimation reduce resting and exercise HR for the same workload and accelerate recovery. | Generally higher vagal HRV and baroreflex sensitivity; acclimation can further enhance autonomic efficiency in heat. | Lower resting BP and attenuated BP reactivity to thermal and psychological stress, especially with regular exercise. | Fitness and acclimation both increase maximal sweat rate and earlier onset; greater WBSL for a given ΔCBT; more uniform regional sweating. | Larger thermally driven EDA components with higher sudomotor capacity, but often lower stress-related reactivity and faster recovery. |

| **Hydration and nutrition (meals, DIT, caffeine, energy drinks, alcohol, thermogenic foods)** | Hypohydration ↑ CBT for a given load and slows cooling; meals and thermogenic foods (capsaicin, caffeine) raise CBT via diet-induced thermogenesis; alcohol in cool environments can ↓ CBT via vasodilation and impaired vasoconstriction. | Dehydration reduces skin blood flow and MST, often ↑ DPG (cooler extremities); meals/alcohol warm skin via vasodilation, especially face and trunk; post-meal hypotension can alter distal perfusion. | Hypohydration, meals, caffeine and energy drinks transiently ↑ HR; alcohol produces complex biphasic HR changes. | Dehydration and stimulants reduce vagal HRV and can ↑ LF/HF; heavy meals transiently depress HRV. | Dehydration can lower resting BP yet ↑ BP variability and orthostatic susceptibility; caffeine and energy drinks ↑ BP acutely; heavy meals may cause postprandial hypotension, especially in older or autonomically impaired adults. | Dehydration ↓ sweat rate and evaporative capacity; caffeine/capsaicin modestly enhance sweating; alcohol interacts with flushing and may disrupt evaporative efficiency. | Skin dryness from dehydration ↓ conductance; caffeine/energy drinks ↑ tonic and phasic EDA; alcohol produces complex, sometimes biphasic, effects on EDA patterns. |

| **Body composition and posture** | Greater subcutaneous fat ↑ insulation and thermal inertia → slower CBT changes and potentially greater stored heat under sustained load; higher surface-to-mass ratio in lean/small individuals → faster CBT shifts. | Obesity and central adiposity distort regional Tsk (cooler extremities, warmer trunk); posture (supine vs upright) redistributes perfusion, changing local Tsk and DPG. | Higher adiposity tends to ↑ resting HR and lower HR reserve; upright posture ↑ HR vs supine/sitting. | Obesity and metabolic syndrome reduce vagal HRV and ↑ sympathetic dominance; supine posture increases HRV relative to standing. | Higher BMI and visceral fat increase BP and hypertension risk; posture changes induce transient BP drops or rises; orthostatic responses amplified in some individuals. | Larger bodies often have higher absolute WBSL, but adiposity can impair local evaporative efficiency; posture-dependent pooling can change local sweat patterns. | Thickened skin and callusing (e.g. soles and palms) reduce EDA signal; posture transitions elicit short-latency SCRs due to orthostatic and arousal effects. |

| **Psychological stress, mental workload, emotional arousal** | Mild/acute stress has small direct CBT effects but can contribute to slow upward drift with prolonged activation. | Sympathetic vasoconstriction in extremities cools hands and feet; facial Tsk may rise with emotional flushing. | Clear tachycardic response to acute stress; magnitude and recovery are highly individual. | Vagal indices fall and LF/HF rises during stress; prolonged or repeated stress can flatten daily HRV patterns. | BP rises during mental stress; large reactivity predicts higher long-term CV risk even when resting BP is normal. | “Emotional sweating” strongly ↑ palmar/plantar sweating independent of thermal load; can contaminate thermoregulatory sweat measures. | Strong ↑ in tonic SCL and SCR frequency/amplitude; major confounder for thermoregulatory EDA unless affect and cognitive load are controlled. |

| **Neurodivergent populations (ASD, ADHD and related)** | Often attenuated CBT amplitude and greater day-to-day variability; CBT peaks and troughs tend to be delayed in ASD/ADHD with pronounced eveningness. | Likely delayed distal warming and altered nocturnal Tsk/DPG patterns; direct data still limited, but circadian phase delays are common. | Higher prevalence of neuro-cardiovascular instability (e.g. POTS, vasovagal episodes); stimulants ↑ HR. | HRV often reduced (lower vagal tone, higher sympathetic drive) and strongly modified by medication. | ADHD cohorts show slightly elevated waking BP and higher rates of elevated BP; ASD cohorts exhibit more labile BP and orthostatic issues. | Atypical sudomotor control; regional sweating and its coupling to thermal load may differ from typical patterns. | ASD: elevated daytime tonic EDA, reduced nocturnal amplitude, weaker alignment with behaviour; ADHD: delayed and altered EDA rhythms; both require careful documentation of diagnosis and medication. |

| **Endocrine and metabolic disease (thyroid, diabetes, insulin resistance, CKD, obesity-related syndrome)** | Hyperthyroidism ↑ CBT and amplifies heat responses; hypothyroidism ↓ CBT and blunts responses; diabetes and metabolic disease alter CBT trajectories under heat/cold. | Diabetic microvascular disease and endocrine pathologies reduce skin perfusion and distort regional Tsk/DPG maps. | Hyperthyroidism raises HR; hypothyroidism lowers HR; diabetic autonomic neuropathy often → resting tachycardia. | Diabetes and metabolic syndrome consistently ↓ vagal HRV and ↑ sympathetic markers; thyroid disease also alters HRV patterns. | Diabetes, CKD and obesity-related syndromes increase BP and variability and reduce nocturnal dipping. | Diabetic and autonomic neuropathies cause regional hypo- or anhidrosis; hyperthyroidism ↑ sweating, hypothyroidism ↓ it. | Autonomic neuropathy lowers or abolishes EDA in affected regions; endocrine states modulate baseline sympathetic tone and reactivity. |

| **Cardiovascular and autonomic disorders, spinal cord injury, obstructive sleep apnoea** | SCI and severe autonomic failure impair heat loss, producing larger CBT excursions; OSA and chronic CV disease alter nocturnal CBT via disturbed sleep and perfusion. | SCI and autonomic lesions create regions with fixed Tsk and absent vasomotor responses; OSA and CV disease alter nocturnal Tsk/DPG through fragmented sleep and altered haemodynamics. | Autonomic failure blunts HR responses; POTS exaggerates HR rise on standing; OSA raises nocturnal HR and increases transient surges. | Markedly reduced HRV and baroreflex sensitivity in autonomic failure and advanced CV disease; OSA fragments HRV rhythms and reduces nocturnal vagal dominance. | Non-dipping or reverse-dipping patterns common in OSA/CKD/advanced hypertension; labile BP and orthostatic hypotension in autonomic neuropathy, Parkinson’s disease, and SCI. | SCI and autonomic failure cause segmental loss of sweating below the lesion with compensatory hyperhidrosis above; OSA and CV disease influence sweat responses via altered autonomic patterning. | Segmental loss of EDA below SCI lesion; exaggerated or dysregulated EDA above lesion; OSA and CV disease alter nocturnal EDA profiles in parallel with HR and BP disturbances. |

| **Medications affecting autonomic and thermoregulatory control (β-blockers, anticholinergics, sedatives, SSRIs/SNRIs, stimulants, others)** | Many psychotropics, anticholinergics, and some anaesthetics narrow the thermoneutral zone or blunt sweating, raising CBT in heat; others impair shivering and cold defence; fever-inducing drugs raise set-point. | Vasoactive agents (β-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, vasodilators) alter skin perfusion; sedatives and centrally acting agents blunt behavioural thermoregulation; anticholinergics can reduce local sweating and modify Tsk. | β-blockers ↓ HR and reactivity; stimulants, thyroid replacement, some antidepressants and antipsychotics ↑ HR. | β-blockers often ↑ HRV by lowering HR; stimulants and many antidepressants/SSRIs ↓ vagal HRV and may ↑ LF/HF; sedatives can dampen variability. | Antihypertensives ↓ BP and can exacerbate orthostatic drops; stimulants and some antidepressants ↑ BP; many drugs modify dipping patterns. | Anticholinergics and some psychotropics suppress sweating; β-blockers and centrally acting drugs are used to treat hyperhidrosis/flushing and alter sudomotor gain; other agents can precipitate hyperhidrosis or night sweats. | Anticholinergics and sedatives ↓ EDA amplitudes; stimulants and some antidepressants/SSRIs ↑ tonic and phasic EDA; many agents change reactivity profiles and should be documented explicitly. |
