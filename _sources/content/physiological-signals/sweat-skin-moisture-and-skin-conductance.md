# Sweat, skin moisture and skin conductance

### Why: the mechanistic reasons 

{ref}`Sweating <label-sweating>` is the body's principal avenue for evaporative heat loss during thermal stress. In warm, dry, and moving air, evaporation can provide 70--80 % of total heat dissipation, becoming the dominant term in the {ref}`heat-balance equation <label-heat-balance-equation>` when convective and radiant avenues are constrained {cite:p}`havenith_individualized_2001,nadel_peripheral_1971,stephenson_circadian_1984`. The {ref}`onset <label-onset>`, rate, and distribution of sweat secretion are governed by sympathetic {ref}`cholinergic activation <label-cholinergic-pathways>` of eccrine glands, integrating both core- and skin-temperature drives {cite:p}`arens_skins_2006,shibasaki_mechanisms_2010`.

Local sweat rate (LSR) therefore represents the available evaporative capacity at a specific site, while the spatial recruitment pattern of active glands indicates how efficiently the body scales evaporative cooling during sustained or repeated heat loads {cite:p}`havenith_individualized_2001,kondo_regional_1998`.

{ref}`Effective evaporation <label-effective-evaporation>` depends not only on secretion rate but also on skin wettedness, air velocity, humidity, and clothing permeability, which together determine the fraction of sweat that actually evaporates versus accumulates or drips {cite:p}`havenith_relative_1990,noauthor_iso_2004`).

Electrodermal activity (EDA), also termed galvanic skin response (GSR), provides an indirect index of sweat-duct filling and sudomotor nerve activity measured via small conductance changes. Because eccrine glands act as variable resistors when filled with electrolyte, EDA reflects both thermoregulatory sweating and non-thermal sympathetic {ref}`arousal <label-arousal>` {cite:p}`benedek_continuous_2010,boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. In thermal-physiology protocols, combining sweat-rate or skin-moisture measures with EDA links {ref}`autonomic thermoeffector output <label-autonomic-thermoeffector-output>` to perceptual and emotional processes, allowing integrated assessment of heat strain, thermal comfort, and {ref}`sympathetic load <label-sympathetic-load>` {cite:p}`critchley_review_2002,gerrett_sweat_2018`.

### How: sensor types for measurement 

Quantifying sweating and skin conductance requires methods that differ in physical principle, temporal resolution, and calibration rigour. Each technique provides a different window onto evaporative heat loss or sudomotor activation, from direct local sweat flux to indirect indices of gland filling and surface moisture.

**Local sweat rate (LSR, ventilated-capsule method).** The gold-standard technique measures water-vapour flux from a small, sealed skin area ventilated with dry air. The vapour-density difference between inlet and outlet streams, determined using dew-point or infrared hygrometers, yields instantaneous LSR in mg·cm⁻²·min⁻¹ {cite:p}`crawshaw_effect_1975,havenith_individualized_2001,nadel_differential_1973,patterson_human_1998`. The system provides continuous, quantitative sweat-flux data, though it requires careful sealing and may slightly alter local evaporation.

**Near-skin humidity and skin moisture probes.** Simpler approaches employ miniature dew-point or capacitive (dielectric) sensors positioned a few mm above the surface to capture relative humidity in the skin microclimate {cite:p}`noauthor_iso_2004`). Readings are sensitive to contact pressure and ambient humidity drift, thus benefiting from standardised positioning and calibration against LSR.

**Absorbent-patch gravimetry and chemical analysis.** For integrated sweat-rate and composition data, absorbent pads or polymer films of known surface area are pre-weighed, applied for fixed intervals, and re-weighed post-collection; extracted sweat is analysed for electrolytes via ion chromatography {cite:p}`baker_validity_2014,baker_comparison_2009`. This method captures ionic composition and glandular {ref}`adaptation <label-adaptation>` but lacks temporal resolution.

**Colorimetric and microfluidic sweat patches.** Flexible polymer sensors containing hydrochromic dyes or serpentine microchannels visualise cumulative sweat volume and dynamics in real time {cite:p}`koh_soft_2016,madhu_wearable_2025,reeder_waterproof_2019,yu_sweat_2020`. The colour intensity or length-of-fill correlates with sweat volume with latency of 1--3 s. These patches are inexpensive and well-suited for field or wearable applications, though quantitative use requires calibration against ventilated-capsule data and correction for evaporative loss beneath the patch.

**Whole-body sweat loss (WBSL).** Global evaporative water loss is determined gravimetrically from the change in nude body mass before and after exposure, corrected for fluid intake and urine output, and normalised to body-surface area {cite:p}`cheuvront_dehydration_2014`. Although WBSL integrates all regional fluxes, it lacks spatial or temporal resolution and is best interpreted alongside local measures.

**Electrodermal activity (EDA).** EDA quantifies {ref}`sympathetic activation <label-sympathetic-load>` of eccrine sweat glands via changes in skin conductance between Ag/AgCl electrodes, typically at a constant 0.5 V DC and 4--32 Hz sampling {cite:p}`benedek_continuous_2010,boucsein_electrodermal_2012,critchley_review_2002`. The signal comprises a slowly varying {ref}`tonic <label-tonic>` component (skin-conductance level, SCL) and rapid {ref}`phasic <label-phasic>` responses (skin-conductance responses, SCRs).

### Where: body sites of measurement

Sweating and electrodermal responses show strong topographical variation that reflects eccrine‐gland density, local skin temperature, and sympathetic innervation. Gland density ranges from fewer than 100 glands·cm⁻² on the trunk (e.g., \~64 on the back) to more than \~250--550 glands cm⁻² on {ref}`palmar <label-spatial-orientation>` and plantar skin {cite:p}`baker_physiology_2019,taylor_regional_2013`. During passive or low-metabolic heat exposures typical of sedentary laboratory studies, the highest thermoregulatory sweat rates occur on the forehead, upper back, chest, and thigh, whereas the {ref}`distal <label-longitudinal-position>` limbs contribute less to total evaporative heat loss {cite:p}`coull_body_2021`.

**Local sweat-rate sites**. Ventilated capsules and absorbent patches are most often applied to the forearm, chest, upper back, or thigh, which combine good attachment stability with representative thermoregulatory behaviour.\
Forearm placement remains the field standard due to historical comparability and low motion {ref}`artefact <label-artefact>`, although biological variation between repeated trials can approach \~ 20--25 % CV {cite:p}`kenefick_biological_2012`. Trunk regions, particularly the back and chest, show higher reproducibility and smaller inter-individual variance than distal sites (CV \< 25 % under passive heating) when measured by the ventilated-capsule method {cite:p}`rutherford_regional_2021`. Technical-absorbent or polymer-patch methods produce equivalent {ref}`steady-state <label-thermal-steady-state>` flux estimates on these regions within \~10 % bias relative to capsule data {cite:p}`morris_comparison_2013`.

**Skin-moisture and wettedness probes.** Capacitance or near-skin-humidity sensors are best mounted on flat, hairless surfaces such as the ventral forearm, upper arm, or upper chest, where contact pressure and curvature {ref}`artefacts <label-artefact>` are minimal. These areas offer stable microclimates suitable for repeated recordings, whereas hair-bearing or curved surfaces (scalp, axillae, abdomen) introduce local humidity pockets that confound steady-state readings {cite:p}`taylor_regional_2013`.

**Electrodermal-activity sites.** Electrodermal-activity electrodes detect sweat-duct filling and sympathetic activation. The palmar and plantar surfaces exhibit the highest gland densities and the largest phasic conductance responses but are also strongly influenced by emotional or cognitive stimuli {cite:p}`hossain_comparison_2022`. For thermoregulatory studies, ventral forearm, upper arm, or upper chest placements are preferred, as they predominantly reflect thermal sympathetic drive rather than affective arousal.

**Confounding regions.** Certain regions, such as face, axillae, abdomen, and palms, may show sweating driven by non-thermal pathways (emotional {cite:p}`harker_psychological_2013,wohlrab_hyperhidrosis_2023`, gustatory {cite:p}`laskawi_gustatory_1999`, or visceral). Facial sweating contributes substantially to total evaporative heat loss {cite:p}`obrien_quantification_2013`, yet remains technically challenging to monitor continuously due to motion, airflow, and cosmetic interference {cite:p}`he_association_2025`.

### Agreeability across sensor types

Agreement between sweat-measurement systems depends on sensing principle, airflow characteristics, surface sealing, and temporal resolution. Comparative work shows that ventilated-capsule and technical-absorbent techniques yield closely matched steady-state local sweat rates under passive heating, with group-level bias typically \< 10 % and limits of agreement around ± 0.3 mg·cm⁻²·min⁻¹ {cite:p}`morris_comparison_2013`. Reliability is region-specific: trunk sites (upper back/chest) show the highest repeatability (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient \~ 0.85), whereas forearm is lower (\~ 0.60) when measured during passive heating {cite:p}`rutherford_regional_2021`. Across repeated trials, analytical variance is dominated by biological variability plus subtle differences in capsule flow, seal integrity, and leak checks {cite:p}`kenefick_biological_2012`.

For near-skin humidity and skin-moisture probes, agreement improves when sensors are cross-calibrated against ventilated capsules under controlled, low-air-movement conditions and when pressure/occlusion are standardised. However, ambient air movement, condensation, and probe pressure can introduce appreciable error, so these systems are best treated as trend measures or calibrated surrogates of local sweat flux in sedentary laboratory settings {cite:p}`noauthor_iso_2004`.

Microfluidic/colorimetric patches show good concordance with capsule-derived flux once calibrated in situ during mild heat stress; typical absolute errors reported are on the order of RMSE \~ 0.2--0.4 mg·cm⁻²·min⁻¹{cite:p}`koh_soft_2016,reeder_waterproof_2019`. Practical limitations include small air gaps or adhesive lift, which can under-collect sweat, and uncertainty from direct evaporation beneath the patch.

For electrodermal activity (EDA), signals recorded with constant-voltage or constant-current amplifiers are generally interchangeable when normalised within-subject and recorded with standardised electrode materials/placements; variability is driven more by electrode-skin interface (cleaning, gel/electrolyte, inter-electrode distance) than by the electronics themselves {cite:p}`benedek_continuous_2010,benedek_decomposition_2010,boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. Electrode-gel composition, surface cleaning, and inter-electrode distance contribute most measurement variability rather than electronics per se.

Overall, under controlled, sedentary laboratory conditions with careful calibration and placement, inter-method bias between established sweat-monitoring approaches (ventilated capsule, technical absorbent, calibrated microfluidics) is typically within \~ ± 10--15 %, with the remaining spread explained by biological variance and local setup details.

### Known confounders and modifiers

Sweat and electrodermal responses vary widely across individuals and conditions. Both are shaped by a combination of intrinsic physiological traits and extrinsic experimental factors that influence sudomotor drive, gland sensitivity, {ref}`evaporative efficiency <label-effective-evaporation>`, or recording fidelity. The following modifiers should be considered when designing or comparing studies under sedentary or mild-activity thermal exposures.

**Circadian influences.** Sweating follows a diurnal rhythm coordinated with core body temperature and sympathetic tone. The onset and gain of the sweating response are lowest in the morning and highest in the late afternoon to early evening, paralleling the daily CBT maximum {cite:p}`aoki_circadian_1997,stephenson_circadian_1984`. Under constant-routine conditions, local sweat rate and electrodermal activity both show afternoon elevations of \~15--25 %, attributed to circadian modulation of hypothalamic sympathetic output {cite:p}`tayefeh_circadian_1998`. Sleep restriction or circadian misalignment dampens this rhythm, delaying sweating onset and reducing amplitude of both thermoregulatory and phasic EDA components {cite:p}`timbal_circadian_1975`. Electrodermal and thermoeffector activity follow clear circadian organisation: EDA amplitude increases during sleep and exhibits a strong 24-h rhythm in controlled settings {cite:p}`kim_contrast_2018,sano_quantitative_2014`. Late-night light exposure or delayed {ref}`chronotype <label-chronotype>` shifts can attenuate the distal warming and sweating responses that normally precede sleep, altering both thermal comfort and circadian alignment {cite:p}`czeisler_stability_1999,krauchi_functional_2000`.

**Sex.** Men generally produce higher absolute sweat rates than women under the same heat load, mainly because of greater output per active gland rather than gland-number differences {cite:p}`gagnon_sex-related_2008,gagnon_sex_2013,gagnon_sex_2012,gagnon_does_2012`. Women, in turn, rely more heavily on cutaneous {ref}`vasodilation <label-vasodilation>` for heat dissipation. This disparity is not due to an anatomical lack of sweat glands; in fact, because of their typically smaller body surface area, women actually possess a higher overall density of eccrine sweat glands than men. The reduced sweating response in females is driven by a significantly lower sweat output per individual gland and a reduced peripheral cholinergic responsiveness {cite:p}`baker_physiology_2019`. Consequently, women are often characterized as more \"efficient\" sweaters during resting heat exposure {cite:p}`yanovich_sex_2020`. They tend to activate a higher percentage of their available sweat glands but secrete less overall fluid, which helps them avoid \"wasted sweating\", where excess sweat pools and drips off the skin without contributing to evaporative cooling, while still achieving effective thermal balance in moderately hot environments.

**Sex hormones and reproductive status.** Menstrual {ref}`phase <label-phase>`, oral-contraceptive use, and menopausal status primarily affect sweating by shifting the core-temperature threshold for activating heat-loss mechanisms {cite:p}`lei_exercise_2019,smith_body_2012,stachenfeld_estrogen_2000`. Progesterone's thermogenic action elevates the hypothalamic {ref}`set-point <label-thermoregulatory-set-point>` by roughly 0.4 °C in the luteal phase {cite:p}`terjung_reproductive_2014,israel_thermogenic_1950`, reflected in higher oesophageal thresholds for sweating (38.0 ± 0.3 °C vs 37.3 ± 0.1 °C in the follicular phase) {cite:p}`stachenfeld_estrogen_2000,stephenson_menstrual_1985`.

Exogenous hormones exert similar effects. Combined OCPs raise the sweating threshold {cite:p}`grucza_influence_1993,sunderland_effect_2003`, consistent with progestin-mediated inhibition of warm-sensitive neurons {cite:p}`nakayama_action_1975`. During the active high-hormone pill week, the oral-temperature threshold increases to \~ 37.15 ± 0.11 °C compared with the placebo week {cite:p}`charkoudian_modification_1997`. Oestrogen counteracts this elevation: unopposed progestin yields a higher threshold (38.07 ± 0.17 °C), whereas combined oestrogen--progestin treatment lowers it (37.46 ± 0.18 °C), confirming oestrogen's cooling influence and antagonism to progestin {cite:p}`stachenfeld_estrogen_2000,stephenson_esophageal_1999`.

In postmenopausal women, oestrogen replacement similarly reduces sweating thresholds, while the addition of progestin abolishes this reduction {cite:p}`brooks_chronic_1997,tankersley_estrogen_1992`. Together, these findings demonstrate that progesterone elevates, and oestrogen lowers, the thermoregulatory operating point; an antagonistic interplay that underlies sex and hormonal differences in sudomotor control.

**Age.** Sweat-gland output and responsiveness decline progressively with ageing. Comparative studies report 30--40 % lower maximal sweat rates between 20 and 60 years, even under identical heat loads {cite:p}`inoue_exercise-_1999,inoue_sex_2016`. This reduction arises from atrophy of secretory coils, reduced cholinergic sensitivity, and impaired {ref}`cutaneous vasodilation <label-vasodilation>`. Consequently, older adults show slower sweat onset and reduced regional uniformity, especially on the limbs and back. EDA amplitude and reactivity likewise diminish with age, reflecting lower sympathetic-skin response gain {cite:p}`shibasaki_mechanisms_2010`.

**Fitness and {ref}`acclimation <label-acclimation>`.** Aerobic training and repeated heat exposure both enhance sudomotor sensitivity and evaporative efficiency. Trained or acclimated individuals begin sweating at a lower core temperature and show steeper sweat rate--temperature slopes {cite:p}`havenith_relative_1990,periard_adaptations_2015`. Glandular recruitment expands, and the ionic composition of sweat (particularly Na⁺ and Cl⁻) shifts toward lower concentrations, reflecting ductal reabsorption adaptation. EDA studies show increased tonic levels during {ref}`heat acclimation <label-acclimation>`, consistent with heightened cholinergic activity {cite:p}`gagnon_does_2012`.

**Body composition.** Subcutaneous adiposity insulates and increases {ref}`heat storage <label-heat-storage>` requirements, typically delaying sweating and reducing heat-loss effectiveness under heat stress {cite:p}`kenny_heat_2010,morrissey_impact_2021`.

**Hydration.** Around \~2% hypohydration, sweating and {ref}`skin blood flow <label-cutaneous-blood-flow>` are reduced and evaporative heat loss becomes less effective, accelerating {ref}`heat storage <label-heat-storage>`; functional impairments increase with greater deficits {cite:p}`kenefick_drinking_2018,kenefick_physiological_2016,sawka_hypohydration_2015`.

**Hidromeiosis (sweat gland fatigue).** Prolonged or repeated wetting of the skin surface suppresses eccrine gland output through a local feedback mechanism: accumulated sweat within the duct creates an osmotic or mechanical signal that progressively reduces secretory rate, a phenomenon termed hidromeiosis {cite:p}`candas_influence_1979,nadel_effect_1973`. Under sustained passive heat exposure in humid conditions, local sweat rates can decline by 20--50 % relative to peak values even as core temperature remains elevated, producing a dissociation between CBT-based thermoregulatory drive and measured sweat output {cite:p}`candas_influence_1979`. Hidromeiosis is most pronounced at sites with poor natural evaporation (occluded by clothing or sensor hardware), and is minimised by ventilated measurement conditions and by wiping the skin surface periodically. Protocols that run longer than 60 min in warm, humid conditions should monitor for hidromeiosis-related decline in LSR, and distinguish it from true reduction in sudomotor drive by cross-referencing with CBT and EDA data.

**Food intake and stimulants.** Postprandial {ref}`thermogenesis <label-thermogenesis>` modestly elevates metabolic {ref}`heat production <label-thermogenesis>` for several hours and can slightly raise core temperature, transiently lowering the load needed to trigger sweating {cite:p}`dalessio_thermic_1988`. Capsaicin (TRPV1 activation) elicits gustatory sweating via trigeminal sympathetic pathways {cite:p}`drummond_mechanisms_1995,kawakami_brain_2016`. Caffeine can modulate thermoregulatory responses in the heat, but effects on sweating and core temperature are heterogeneous across studies and doses {cite:p}`hunt_caffeine_2021,li_effects_2024`. Nicotine increases sympathetic arousal, reflected in elevated EDA, while its effects on heat-evoked sweating remain less well characterised {cite:p}`ho_rapid_2020`. Alcohol at low-to-moderate doses (0.5--1.0 g·kg⁻¹ body weight) causes peripheral vasodilation that raises mean skin temperature by approximately 1--2 °C and increases whole-body sweat rate by 10--25 % above baseline; this is followed by enhanced evaporative cooling that can lower core temperature by 0.3--0.5 °C, progressively impairing thermoregulatory control {cite:p}`yoda_effects_2005,shirreffs_effect_1997`. For protocols with any social or residential context, alcohol consumed within 12 h of a session should be documented at the session level.


**Antiperspirant use.** Aluminium-based antiperspirants — the most widely used class — form a physical plug in the eccrine duct opening that can reduce local axillary sweat rate by 20--60 % and persist for 24--48 h after a single application {cite:p}`quatrale_mechanism_1981,laden_antiperspirant_1999`. Because antiperspirant use is near-universal in adult populations (daily use in > 80 % of adults in most Western countries), it represents a systematic and often undocumented modifier of local sweat rate measurements. Its effect is site-specific — axillary measurements are most affected, while forearm, chest, and back measurements are typically unaffected — but participants who apply antiperspirant to non-axillary sites (e.g. sports formulations applied to back or chest) will show reduced LSR at those sites as well. Participants should be instructed to abstain from antiperspirant application to measurement sites for at least 48 h before the session, and compliance should be documented as a session-level field {cite:p}`baker_physiology_2019`.

**Psychological and cognitive arousal**. Non-thermal sympathetic activation can strongly modulate EDA and palmar sweating. {ref}`Mental workload <label-cognitive-or-mental-workload>`, anxiety, or startle evoke rapid phasic conductance spikes and transient local sweating even under thermoneutral conditions {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012,critchley_review_2002,edelberg_electrical_1972`. These responses are most pronounced at glabrous sites such as the palms, soles, and forehead -- regions densely innervated by sympathetic cholinergic fibers {cite:p}`taylor_regional_2013`, so they can obscure thermoregulatory signals if used for measurement. Non-glabrous placements (forearm, upper chest) minimise this interference.

**Cold exposure.** While the existing confounders literature focuses primarily on warm exposures, cold environments relevant to building research (e.g., offices below 19 °C, cold-side asymmetric radiation, winter conditions) produce distinct sudomotor signatures. Peripheral sympathetic vasoconstriction during cold suppresses distal skin temperature and reduces EDA amplitude at palmar sites due to reduced gland perfusion, while tonic SCL may remain elevated through sympathetically mediated non-thermoregulatory arousal pathways {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. Local sweat rate at thermoregulatory sites (forearm, chest) decreases markedly in cold conditions as sudomotor drive is withdrawn, making LSR a poor index of sympathetic load in cooling protocols. The thermal direction of the exposure (heating vs. cooling) should therefore be explicitly considered when interpreting EDA and LSR signals, and the choice of measurement site should be justified in relation to expected thermoregulatory patterning under cold conditions.

**Neurodivergent populations.** Autonomic dysregulation in autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) can alter sudomotor profiles. In ASD, studies report elevated daytime tonic conductance, reduced nocturnal EDA amplitude, and weaker coupling between EDA and behavioural states, suggesting atypical sympathetic modulation {cite:p}`chong_sleep_2021,schiltz_electrodermal_2022`. In adults with ADHD, there is evidence of delayed and altered {ref}`circadian rhythms <label-circadian-rhythm>` in body temperature and activity patterns {cite:p}`bijlenga_body_2013` and more broadly in neurophysiological responsiveness {cite:p}`imeraj_altered_2012`.

**Post-viral dysautonomia and long COVID.** Post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) can impair sudomotor function through small-fibre neuropathy and autonomic dysregulation, producing anhidrosis, hyperhidrosis, or abnormal EDA reactivity that is unrelated to the thermal exposure under study {cite:p}`dani_autonomic_2021,davis_long_2023`. Because these symptoms are invisible on standard screening, participants with a COVID-19 history should be directly asked about persistent sweating abnormalities; if present, their sweat and EDA data may require separate treatment.

**Underlying medical conditions and medications.** Neurological and endocrine disorders can substantially alter thermoregulatory sweating by disrupting sympathetic {ref}`cholinergic pathways <label-cholinergic-pathways>` or shifting central set-points. Autonomic neuropathies, classically in diabetes mellitus, Parkinson's disease, and after spinal-cord injury, damage postganglionic fibers innervating eccrine glands and produce regional hypohidrosis/anhidrosis, diminishing evaporative heat loss under environmental heat stress {cite:p}`cheshire_thermoregulatory_2016,habek_sudomotor_2022,low_evaluation_2004,reitz_sympathetic_2002`. Endocrine disorders likewise modify {ref}`heat balance <label-body-heat-balance>`: thyroid excess increases metabolic heat production, while deficiency reduces it; changes that can raise or lower the threshold load required to trigger sweating {cite:p}`cheshire_thermoregulatory_2016,iwen_effects_2018`. Medications also modulate {ref}`sudomotor activity <label-sudomotor-activity>`: anticholinergics directly suppress eccrine secretion via muscarinic blockade; β-blockers and centrally acting agents (e.g., propranolol, clonidine) are used in selected hyperhidrosis or flushing contexts and can alter heat-loss responses -- hence essential careful medication screening in heat-stress studies {cite:p}`glaser_use_2015`.

**Measurement artefacts.** Measurement artefacts can rival physiological variance in magnitude. In sweat-rate systems, leaks or condensation within tubing alter humidity gradients; in EDA, variations in skin cleaning, electrode pressure, and gel conductivity produce drift and false phasic peaks {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. Skin cleaning with alcohol or abrasion can transiently increase local sweating by stimulating mechanoreceptors. Topical products applied to the skin in the days before measurement — including moisturisers, sunscreens, cosmetics, and medical preparations — can alter eccrine duct patency, skin surface conductivity, and infrared emissivity {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012,macrae_skin_2018`. For EDA specifically, residual product on the skin surface changes electrode-skin impedance and can produce artifactually elevated or suppressed baseline conductance levels. For skin temperature measurements using infrared techniques, lotions and cosmetics lower apparent emissivity in a site-specific, unpredictable manner. Participants should be instructed to avoid applying products to measurement sites for a defined period (typically 12--24 h) before the session, and this instruction and its documentation should be included in the session-level protocol.

### Data handling methods

#### Sensor calibration

Accurate quantification of sweat and EDA signals requires pre- and post-session calibration to correct for drift and environmental bias.

**Ventilated-capsule systems**. Flow meters should be verified (0.5--1.0 L·min⁻¹) and dew-point sensors checked for linearity across 0--100 % RH. Zero-flow offsets are recorded at the start of each session to remove {ref}`baseline <label-baseline>` drift {cite:p}`noauthor_iso_2004`. Capacitive or dielectric probes are calibrated against saturated-salt humidity standards to map raw voltage to relative humidity.

**Local sweat rate (LSR)**. Some wearables require a set stabilisation period within an environmental chamber prior to data collection. This period is used to assess an individual \"offset number\", which is later subtracted from the raw score during analysis to yield standardised LSR values {cite:p}`relf_reliability_2019`. For colorimetric microfluidic (epifluidic) devices, calibration is integrated into the device hardware through the printing of reference markers (e.g., a white centree dot and four black crosses) {cite:p}`koh_soft_2016`. These markers enable software to perform automatic white {ref}`balancing <label-balancing>` and position calibration, eliminating measurement errors caused by varying lighting conditions (daylight vs shadow) or device orientation {cite:p}`choi_skin-interfaced_2018,koh_soft_2016`.

**Sweat composition and biochemical analysis**. Calibration of analytical equipment is essential for quantifying sweat electrolytes. Handheld ion-selective electrode (ISE) analysers require frequent calibration (approximately every 10 measurements) to prevent instrument drift {cite:p}`baker_validity_2014`. A 2-point calibration is used for sodium with 150 ppm and 2000 ppm standard solutions, while a 1-point calibration (150 ppm) is used for potassium {cite:p}`baker_validity_2014`. Conductivity-based systems are calibrated using distilled cold water according to manufacturer guidelines before samples are processed {cite:p}`relf_reliability_2019`. For patch-based collection, background sodium, chloride, and other electrolyte concentrations are determined from unexposed patches and used as correction factors {cite:p}`klous_sweat_2020`. Colorimetric assays for biomarkers like chloride are calibrated by filling devices with known stock solutions (e.g., 25, 50, 75, and 100 mM) to produce a calibration curve or reference colour dial for image-based quantitation {cite:p}`reeder_waterproof_2019`.

**Electrodermal activity (EDA)**. Calibration for EDA involves electronic standardisation to manage the wide dynamic range of skin conductance. Wheatstone bridge circuits can be used to cancel out (or \"balance\") the large tonic skin conductance level so that small phasic responses can be resolved {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. Using adjustable resistors--such as a 10-turn potentiometer where each complete turn cancels out 10 μmhos--the experimenter balances the bridge to achieve an optimal amplifier sensitivity (e.g., 1 mV/cm) {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. Modern high-resolution analog-to-digital converters often bypass the need for this separate circuitry, instead using adaptive gain controls or high-pass {ref}`filtering <label-filtering>` to compress the dynamic range and manage slow-varying baseline drift {cite:p}`tronstad_current_2022`.

#### Data cleaning and correction

#### Local Sweat Rate (LSR)

**Filtering for {ref}`noise <label-noise>`.** Data integrity for LSR begins with rigorous skin preparation; sites are cleaned with alcohol and deionised water, then dried thoroughly to remove sebum and residual minerals {cite:p}`akbar_sudomotor_2023,baker_physiological_2020,klous_sweat_2020`. To \"flush\" the sweat ducts of old sweat and surface contaminants, the first 20--30 minutes of sweat is often wiped away before actual collection begins. To filter out \"reactive error\" and prevent hidromeiosis (suppression of sweating caused by skin wettedness), ventilated capsules are supplied with dry air or nitrogen at flow rates typically between 0.4 and 2.0 L/min {cite:p}`baker_physiology_2019,kondo_regional_1998,lei_influence_2017,morris_comparison_2013,mundel_thermoregulatory_2020`. In patch-based collection, noise from solid skin contaminants is filtered via centrifugation or a syringe compression method to isolate pure sweat fluid from the absorbent material {cite:p}`akbar_sudomotor_2023,baker_physiology_2019,klous_sweat_2020`.

**{ref}`Normalisation <label-normalization>`.** LSR is standardly normalised gravimetrically by calculating the mass of the sweat (wet patch minus dry patch mass) divided by the skin surface area and the duration of application, expressed as *mg⋅cm⁻²⋅min⁻¹* {cite:p}`klous_sweat_2020,smith_body_2011`. To account for session-to-session variability, regional sweat rates may be standardised against the individual's Gross Sweat Loss (GSL) {cite:p}`smith_body_2011`. Furthermore, mathematical modelling uses sensitivity coefficients to weight skin areas based on their thermal sensor density {cite:p}`havenith_individualized_2001,nadel_differential_1973`.

**Discarded data.** In exercise physiology studies, data from the first and last 20 minutes of an exercise bout are often discarded in patch methods because they lack the temporal resolution to track the rapid onset and decay of sweating seen in continuous hygrometry {cite:p}`morris_comparison_2013,taylor_regional_2013`. Additionally, some protocols discard the first 20--30 minutes of sweating to \"flush\" the sweat ducts of old sweat and contaminants {cite:p}`akbar_sudomotor_2023`. Duplicate measurements showing a major variance are handled by discarding the outlier and retaining the value best representing the area activation {cite:p}`baker_physiology_2019,poirier_local_2016`. In sedentary studies, the initial minutes may show an overshoot effect depending on the change in exposure from baseline; therefore, this could be a specific part of the analysis.

**Typical ranges.** Regional variations are significant: under passive heat exposure at rest, the forehead ranges from 0.99 to 1.71 mg⋅cm⁻²⋅min⁻¹, the upper back from 0.59 to 1.20 mg⋅cm⁻²⋅min⁻¹, and the limbs approximately 0.12 mg⋅cm⁻²⋅min⁻¹ {cite:p}`smith_body_2012,taylor_regional_2013`.

#### Skin Wettedness ({math}`\omega_{\text{max}}`)

**Filtering for noise.** Skin wettedness is a derived mathematical metric representing the proportion of the body surface covered by sweat. The noise is filtered by identifying the critical ambient vapour pressure inflection point from core temperature graphs during a humidity ramp protocol, which isolates the exact transition from compensable to uncompensable heat stress {cite:p}`baker_physiology_2019,mundel_thermoregulatory_2020,ravanelli_maximum_2018`.

**Normalisation.** {math}`\omega_{\text{max}}` is expressed as a dimensionless ratio (0.00 to 1.00), where 1.00 represents a skin surface 100% saturated with sweat {cite:p}`baker_physiology_2019,ravanelli_maximum_2018`.

**Discarded data.** Calculations focus on the upper physiological limit of evaporative heat loss; therefore, the mathematical derivation of {math}`\omega_{\text{max}}` requires identifying the specific inflection point where the body can no longer maintain thermal balance, rendering data below this threshold as strictly compensable baseline {cite:p}`mundel_thermoregulatory_2020`.

**Typical ranges.** While traditional ISO models assume a {math}`\omega_{\text{max}}` of 0.85 for unacclimated individuals and 1.00 following successful heat acclimation , recent empirical data show values ranging from 0.72 in untrained/unacclimated individuals, to 0.84 in trained/unacclimated individuals, and up to 0.95 following aerobic training combined with heat acclimation {cite:p}`candas_human_1979,periard_adaptations_2015`.

#### Total Sweat Loss (Whole-Body Sweat Rate - WBSR)

**Normalisation.** WBSR is expressed in litres per hour (L/h) or grams per minute (g/min). It is rigorously normalised through corrections for mass errors, including fluid intake, urine voiding, and metabolic/respiratory mass loss due to gas exchange {cite:p}`baker_physiology_2019,mundel_thermoregulatory_2020`.

**Typical ranges.** Rates typically range from \~0.2 to 0.4 L/h at rest or under mild conditions, up to 3.0 L/h across a wide range of activities, with strenuous endurance exercise in the heat occasionally eliciting maximal sustainable rates approaching 4.0 L/h {cite:p}`baker_physiological_2020,kenefick_physiological_2016`.

#### Electrodermal Activity (EDA)

**Filtering for noise.** EDA signals are resampled (e.g., at 8 Hz from 100 Hz) to improve processing efficiency {cite:p}`hossain_comparison_2022`. Noise is filtered using low-pass Butterworth filters with cutoff frequencies between 0.6 Hz and 10 Hz to remove high-frequency artefacts {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012,hossain_comparison_2022`. High-pass filtering is applied to remove the slow-shifting tonic baseline (SCL) to isolate phasic responses {cite:p}`braithwaite_jason_j_guide_2015,tronstad_current_2022` (see Derived parameters).

**Normalisation.** Logarithmic and square root transformations are used to address data skewness and Poisson distributions {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. Range correction scales data between an individual's minimum and maximum reactivity (0.0 to 1.0) to facilitate inter-individual comparison. Signals may also be detrended to eliminate slow shifts from thermoregulation {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012`.

**Discarded data.** The first and last 10 minutes of tasks are often discarded to eliminate the effects of physiological acclimatisation and anticipation anxiety {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. Any segments containing artefacts from movement, speech, or deep respiration (e.g., sighs) are rejected {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012,braithwaite_jason_j_guide_2015,tronstad_current_2022`.

**Typical ranges.** Tonic SCL generally falls between 2 and 20 µS {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012,society_for_psychophysiological_research_ad_hoc_committee_on_electrodermal_measures_publication_2012`. Phasic SCRs are registered using a detection criterion of 0.01 to 0.05 µS {cite:p}`braithwaite_jason_j_guide_2015`, with frequencies typically ranging from 1 to over 20 SCRs/min, depending on the level of autonomic arousal {cite:p}`boucsein_electrodermal_2012,braithwaite_jason_j_guide_2015`.

#### Derived parameters

Depending on study design and research aim, several key parameters are derived from the preprocessed sweat-rate and electrodermal signals. These parameters describe both the magnitude and temporal dynamics of {ref}`sudomotor activity <label-sudomotor-activity>` at local and whole-body levels.

For local and whole-body sweating, the following parameters are calculated:

- <u>Sweat-onset temperature (°C).</u> The core or skin temperature at which sweat rate first rises above baseline by \> 0.01 mg·cm⁻²·min⁻¹, indicating activation of eccrine glands.
- <u>Maximum sweat rate (mg·cm⁻²·min⁻¹). The</u> highest value reached during exposure; represents glandular output capacity.
- <u>Sweating sensitivity (mg·cm⁻²·min⁻¹·°C⁻¹).</u> The slope of the sweat-rate--core-temperature relation after onset; reflects {ref}`thermoeffector gain <label-thermoeffector-gain>`.
- <u>Total sweat output (mg·cm⁻²).</u> The time-integrated area under the sweat-rate curve; quantifies cumulative evaporative loss for a given site or exposure period.
- <u>Whole-body sweat loss (WBSL, kg·h⁻¹ or L·h⁻¹).</u> Calculated as:
```{math}
\text{WBSL} = (m_{\text{pre}} - m_{\text{post}}) + (V_{\text{fluid}} - V_{\text{urine}})
```
The result is normalised by body-surface area using the Du Bois & Du Bois equation. Typical ranges are 0.2--0.6 kg·h⁻¹ at rest and \> 1 kg·h⁻¹ during moderate heat stress, depending on acclimation, clothing, and environment {cite:p}`gagnon_evaporative_2013`.

EDA signals are decomposed into {ref}`tonic <label-tonic>` (SCL) and {ref}`phasic <label-phasic>` (SCR) components to separate {ref}`baseline <label-baseline>` sympathetic tone from discrete bursts of sudomotor nerve activity {cite:p}`benedek_continuous_2010,boucsein_electrodermal_2012`. Typical palmar conductance levels range 1--20 µS, with transient SCR amplitudes of 0.05--0.5 µS and 0--5 events·min⁻¹ under resting to mild-stress conditions.

- <u>Skin conductance level (SCL, µS).</u> Mean tonic conductance over a defined period; represents baseline sympathetic drive.
- <u>ΔSCL (µS).</u> Change in mean SCL between baseline and exposure; expresses magnitude of sympathetic shift.
- <u>Skin conductance response count (SCR·min⁻¹).</u> Number of phasic responses exceeding a threshold (0.01--0.05 µS); reflects frequency of sudomotor bursts.
- <u>Mean SCR amplitude (ΔµS).</u> Average height of detected SCRs; indicates intensity of individual sympathetic discharges.
- <u>Phasic driver / sudomotor-nerve activity (µS·s⁻¹).</u> Output of non-negative deconvolution models (NeuroKit2 {cite:p}`makowski_neurokit2_2021` or Ledalab {cite:p}`benedek_decomposition_2010`); provides a continuous index of sympathetic input.
- <u>Response latency (s).</u> Interval between stimulus and SCR {ref}`onset <label-onset>`; reflects efferent conduction time and gland responsiveness.
- <u>EDA reactivity index (%).</u> (ΔSCL / baseline SCL) × 100; normalised measure for inter-subject comparison.

For circadian or long-term recordings, additional descriptors can be extracted from smoothed SCL time series:

- <u>Amplitude (µS).</u> Difference between daily maximum and minimum; expresses circadian modulation of sympathetic tone.
- <u>Mean level (µS).</u> 24-h average; reflects overall autonomic balance.
- <u>{ref}`Phase <label-phase>` timing (h).</u> Clock time of minimum SCL or maximum SCR frequency; aligns EDA rhythm with core-temperature phase {cite:p}`vittrant_circadian-like_2023`.
