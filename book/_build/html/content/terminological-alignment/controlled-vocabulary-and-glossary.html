Controlled vocabulary and glossary#

This glossary focuses on physiological measurements and related methodological terminology used in thermal-environment research. It does not attempt to reproduce established built-environment terminology for environmental variables such as air temperature, operative temperature, or relative humidity, which are already well standardized elsewhere (e.g., ISO 7726 for physical environmental measurements, ISO 7730 for thermal comfort, and ISO 8996 for metabolic rate determination) [ISO, 1998, ISO, 2005, ISO, 2021]. Instead, it concentrates on terms that are used inconsistently across thermal physiology, environmental ergonomics, and measurement practice, especially where site, method, or derivation substantially affect interpretation.

A number of established glossaries, standards, and terminological frameworks already provide comprehensive definitions across related domains. In thermal physiology, the IUPS Glossary of Terms for Thermal Physiology remains the primary reference for core concepts, symbols, and the heat-balance framework, with the explicit aim of improving precision and uniformity in terminology [IUPS Thermal Commission, 2001, Bligh and Johnson, 1973]. In biometeorology and human–environment interaction, the International Society of Biometeorology (ISB) glossary offers an interdisciplinary vocabulary linking physiological, meteorological, and epidemiological concepts, and directs readers to standards such as ISO 9886 for physiological measurements of thermal strain [ISO, 2004, International Society of Biometeorology, 2018]. For meteorological variables and atmospheric terminology, the American Meteorological Society (AMS) Glossary of Meteorology provides authoritative definitions, while epidemiological terms are covered in sources such as A Dictionary of Epidemiology and classification systems such as the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) [American Meteorological Society, 2024, Porta, 2026, World Health Organization, 2019]. More general scientific and psychological constructs (e.g., perception, stress, adaptation) are defined in domain-specific dictionaries such as the APA Dictionary of Psychology [American Psychological Association, 2024]. In addition, formal ontologies and unit systems (e.g., QUDT) provide machine-readable representations of quantities, units, and dimensions for data standardization [QUDT.org, 2024].

The present glossary does not aim to replicate or systematically extend these existing resources. Rather, it selectively introduces and clarifies terms as they are used within this document, with the primary goal of supporting readability and interpretability. The emphasis is therefore on terms whose meaning depends on measurement protocol, anatomical site, instrumentation, data processing, or reporting conventions, particularly in experimental and field studies of human thermal physiology. Where established definitions exist, these are followed in principle and readers are directed to the original sources for full definitions and formal standardization. The glossary thus functions as a context-specific, measurement-oriented vocabulary that complements, rather than replaces, existing standards and glossaries.

This glossary is organized alphabetically. Use the search field below to filter entries.

A#

Acclimation

Definition
Physiological or behavioural changes occurring within an organism in response to experimentally induced changes in specific climatic factors.

Why it matters
The term distinguishes responses induced under controlled experimental conditions from responses arising from seasonal or natural environmental exposure.

Notes
In thermal physiology, acclimation is typically treated as a form of phenotypic adaptation to a defined environmental stimulus.

Related concepts
Adaptation; Acclimatization

Acclimatization

Definition
Physiological or behavioural changes occurring within the lifetime of an organism that reduce strain caused by changes in the natural climate.

Why it matters
The term separates adaptation to natural environmental exposure from adaptation produced by experimental intervention.

Notes
Its historical use is inconsistent, so explicit definition is especially helpful in environmental and occupational studies.

Related concepts
Adaptation; Acclimation

Acrophase

Unit
Commonly h

Definition
The timing of the peak of a rhythm within its cycle.

Why it matters
Acrophase is useful for describing when a variable reaches its maximum and for comparing rhythm timing across people or conditions.

Notes
Its estimation depends on sampling density, model choice, and whether the rhythm is sufficiently regular.

Reporting
Interpretation should specify how acrophase is estimated and over what observation period.

Related concepts
Circadian rhythm; Phase; Nadir

Adaptation

Definition
Changes that reduce the physiological strain produced by environmental stressors; these changes may be phenotypic (occuring within the lifetime of the organism) or genotypic (by genetic selection).

Why it matters
The term is broader than most experimental studies intend, and unqualified use can obscure whether changes are short-term or long-term.

Notes
In thermal physiology, adaptation is often used for reduced strain under repeated or chronic exposure, but its exact meaning benefits from further specification.

Related concepts
Acclimation; Acclimatization

Aggregation

Definition
The combination of multiple values into a summary measure across time, trials, regions, or participants.

Why it matters
Aggregation strongly affects interpretability because it determines the scale at which a phenomenon is represented.

Notes
Averaging may improve stability but can also conceal meaningful within-person, within-region, or temporal variation.

Reporting
Interpretation is strengthened by stating what has been aggregated and what structure may have been lost in the process.

Related concepts
Feature extraction; Normalisation; Mean skin temperature

Allostatic load

Definition
The cumulative physiological burden associated with repeated or chronic regulatory effort across interacting systems.

Why it matters
The concept provides a framework for interpreting why chronically demanding thermal or behavioural environments may matter even when short-term comfort is preserved.

Notes
Reduced variability, altered endocrine regulation, and persistent autonomic load may all contribute to allostatic burden, but no single marker is sufficient on its own.

Related concepts
Sympathetic load; Arousal; HPA axis

Arousal

Definition
A state of physiological and psychological activation that can influence autonomic, endocrine, and behavioural responses.

Why it matters
Arousal is important because many physiological signals used in thermal studies are also sensitive to non-thermal activation.

Notes
The term can refer to multiple dimensions, including emotional, cognitive, and physiological activation, which should not be assumed to be identical.

Related concepts
Sympathetic load; Cognitive or mental workload; HPA axis

Artefact

Definition
A distortion or spurious feature in a signal that does not represent the physiological process of interest.

Why it matters
Artefacts can create false structure, bias derived metrics, or be mistaken for meaningful events.

Notes
Artefacts may arise from motion, contact loss, breathing, electrical interference, environmental perturbation, or preprocessing choices.

Reporting
Interpretation is improved when artefact criteria and handling procedures are reported explicitly.

Related concepts
Noise; Filtering; Stationarity

Arterial pressure

Unit
Commonly mmHg

Definition
The pressure exerted by blood within the arterial system.

Why it matters
Its regulation is essential because thermal and postural challenges can alter vascular resistance and venous return.

Notes
In thermal physiology, arterial pressure is important not only as a cardiovascular endpoint but as a driver of compensatory reflexes such as the baroreflex.

Reporting
Interpretation is improved when pressure is considered alongside flow, resistance, and thermoeffector responses rather than in isolation.

Related concepts
Cardiovascular regulation; Baroreflex; Peripheral resistance

Arteriovenous anastomoses (AVA)

Definition
Specialized vascular shunts that allow blood to bypass capillary beds, especially in acral regions such as the hands and feet.

Why it matters
They play a major role in distal heat exchange and therefore help explain strong thermal responses at peripheral sites.

Notes
AVA-rich regions are especially important in studies of distal warming, vasoconstriction, and sleep-related thermoregulation.

Reporting
Interpretation should distinguish AVA-rich glabrous sites from non-glabrous skin, as they do not behave identically.

Related concepts
Vasomotor control; Distal–proximal gradient; Skin blood flow

Autonomic heat-loss drive

Definition
The autonomic regulatory tendency to promote heat dissipation through mechanisms such as sweating and cutaneous vasodilation.

Why it matters
The concept is useful for distinguishing the control signal for heat loss from the observable magnitude of the resulting response.

Notes
Heat-loss drive may be present even when measured heat loss is constrained by environment, clothing, or local measurement limitations.

Related concepts
Thermoeffector; Sweating; Vasodilation

Autonomic regulation

Definition
The regulation of physiological function through the autonomic nervous system, including sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways.

Why it matters
It links thermal, cardiovascular, sudomotor, and neuroendocrine responses that are often measured separately in experiments.

Notes
In thermal physiology, autonomic regulation underlies sweating, vasomotor adjustments, cardiac responses, and aspects of circadian and stress-related modulation.

Related concepts
Sympathetic and parasympathetic outflow; Vagal influences; Central autonomic regulation

Autonomic thermoeffector output

Definition
The autonomically mediated physiological output of thermoregulatory effectors such as sweating, vasomotion, or cardiovascular adjustment.

Why it matters
It links central regulation to observable peripheral responses measured in experiments.

Notes
The phrase is useful when discussing thermoeffector activity at the system level rather than the specific mechanism of a single response.

Related concepts
Thermoeffector; Autonomic regulation; Sweating

B#

Balancing

Definition
The design practice of distributing conditions or participant characteristics so that groups or sequences are as comparable as possible.

Why it matters
Balancing reduces the risk that systematic asymmetry drives apparent effects.

Notes
Balancing may be achieved through randomization, blocking, stratification, or sequence design rather than through a single method.

Reporting
Reporting should state what was balanced and by what mechanism.

Related concepts
Randomization; Block design; Stratification

Baroreflex

Definition
A short-latency cardiovascular feedback mechanism that adjusts heart rate and vascular tone in response to changes in arterial pressure.

Why it matters
It is important because thermal stress changes skin blood flow and venous return, thereby altering the cardiovascular control problem that must be stabilised.

Notes
Baroreflex-related influences are one reason HRV changes under heat cannot be interpreted solely as changes in sympathetic dominance.

Reporting
The term is most informative when a feedback-control interpretation is intended and distinguished from generic autonomic activation.

Related concepts
Arterial pressure; Heart rate variability; Cardiovascular regulation

Baseline

Definition
A reference level of a signal or variable measured before a perturbation, event, or analysis interval of interest.

Why it matters
Baseline provides the contextual starting point from which change, onset, recovery, or reactivity are interpreted.

Notes
Baseline values are not neutral by default; they depend on posture, timing, context, prior exposure, and preprocessing choices.

Reporting
It is helpful to define the duration, timing, and conditions used to establish baseline values.

Related concepts
Onset; Recovery; Normalisation; Thermal steady state; Transient thermal response

Between-subject design

Definition
A design in which comparisons are made across different participants allocated to different conditions.

Why it matters
It avoids within-person carryover but is more affected by inter-individual variability than repeated-measures designs.

Notes
Between-subject comparisons depend heavily on allocation quality, group comparability, and adequate sample size.

Reporting
Reporting should describe assignment, comparability procedures, and handling of baseline differences.

Related concepts
Parallel design; Randomization; Blinding

Blinding

Definition
A design feature in which participants, investigators, analysts, or some combination are kept unaware of condition assignment or study hypotheses.

Why it matters
Blinding reduces bias arising from expectation, differential treatment, or selective interpretation.

Notes
In environmental and physiological studies, full blinding may be difficult when exposures are perceptible, but partial blinding may still be possible.

Reporting
Reporting should specify who was blinded, to what information, and how blinding was maintained or limited.

Related concepts
Randomization; Between-subject design

Block design

Definition
A design or allocation strategy in which conditions or assignments are organized into blocks to improve balance or control nuisance variation.

Why it matters
Blocking can reduce confounding by time, order, batch, or participant characteristics.

Notes
The term may refer to experimental structure or randomization strategy, so its meaning should be specified.

Reporting
Reporting should clarify what is being blocked and why.

Related concepts
Randomization; Balancing; Stratification

Body heat balance

Definition
The condition in which total heat gain equals total heat loss, such that no net body heat is stored.

Why it matters
It provides the conceptual basis for interpreting whether the body is thermally stable, compensating, or accumulating heat.

Notes
A state of body heat balance may be achieved with very different physiological costs, including sweating, vasodilation, or cardiovascular strain.

Related concepts
Heat-balance equation; Heat storage; Thermal load; Thermal strain

C#

Calorimetry (direct and indirect)

Definition
A family of methods used to estimate energy expenditure or heat exchange, either by measuring heat directly or by inferring it from gas exchange.

Why it matters
It provides the methodological bridge between metabolism and thermal interpretation.

Notes
Direct calorimetry refers to direct measurement of heat transfer, whereas indirect calorimetry estimates energy expenditure from oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production.

Reporting
Interpretation should state whether calorimetry is direct or indirect and which derived variables are used in subsequent analysis.

Related concepts
Energy expenditure; Heat-balance equation; Resting energy expenditure

Cardiac output (CO)

Unit
Commonly L·min ⁻¹

Definition
The volume of blood pumped by the heart per unit time.

Why it matters
It is a central determinant of convective heat transport and perfusion during thermal stress.

Notes
In heat exposure, cardiac output often increases to support both arterial pressure and cutaneous heat transfer. Important not to confuse with Stroke Volume; the amount of blood volume pumped out of the heart per beat, calculated as the difference between the end-diastolic volume and the end-systolic volume.

Reporting
Interpretation benefits from clarifying whether cardiac output is measured directly, estimated, or inferred from related cardiovascular variables.

Related concepts
Cardiovascular regulation; Chronotropic effort; Peripheral resistance

Cardiovascular regulation

Definition
The integrated control of heart function, vascular resistance, pressure, and flow distribution needed to maintain perfusion and support thermal adjustment.

Why it matters
It links thermoregulatory demands to circulatory effort and therefore helps explain why similar temperatures may impose different physiological costs.

Notes
In thermal physiology, cardiovascular regulation often includes changes in heart rate, cardiac output, peripheral resistance, and pressure control.

Related concepts
Cardiac output; Arterial pressure; Peripheral resistance; Baroreflex

Cardiovascular strain

Definition
The physiological burden placed on the cardiovascular system in response to thermal, environmental, or regulatory demand.

Why it matters
It distinguishes between observed cardiovascular responses and the underlying effort required to maintain perfusion and support thermoregulation.

Notes
Cardiovascular strain may increase even when core temperature or pressure remain stable, reflecting compensatory effort rather than failure.

Related concepts
Cardiovascular workload; Thermal strain; Allostatic load

Cardiovascular workload

Definition
The circulatory effort required to maintain perfusion and support physiological demands, including thermoregulation.

Why it matters
It provides an interpretation layer for why thermal states with similar temperatures may differ in cardiovascular cost.

Notes
The term is broader than heart rate alone and may reflect the combined demands placed on cardiac output, pressure regulation, and vascular control.

Related concepts
Cardiac output; Chronotropic effort; Thermal strain

Central autonomic regulation

Definition
The central neural coordination of autonomic responses across cardiovascular, thermoregulatory, sudomotor, and neuroendocrine systems.

Why it matters
It provides a bridge between central regulation and peripheral physiological outputs.

Notes
The term is broader than any single pathway and is useful when multiple autonomic outputs are considered together.

Related concepts
Autonomic regulation; Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits; Allostatic load

Cholinergic pathways

Definition
Neural pathways in which acetylcholine acts as the primary neurotransmitter.

Why it matters
They are especially relevant in thermal physiology because sympathetic cholinergic activation drives eccrine sweating.

Notes
The term should not be equated simplistically with parasympathetic function, since sympathetic cholinergic pathways are a key exception.

Reporting
Where cholinergic mechanisms are invoked, it is helpful to specify whether the context is autonomic, sudomotor, or broader neurophysiological control.

Related concepts
Autonomic regulation; Sweating; Eccrine glands

Chronotropic effort

Definition
The cardiac effort associated specifically with adjustment of heart rate.

Why it matters
The term is useful when thermal or circulatory demand is met primarily through heart-rate change rather than through stroke-volume adjustment.

Notes
In passive heat exposure, increased chronotropic effort often reflects the cardiovascular cost of supporting heat dissipation while maintaining pressure.

Related concepts
Cardiovascular workload; Heart rate variability; Cardiac output

Chronotype

Definition
A characteristic pattern of preferred or habitual timing of sleep, activity, and associated circadian phase.

Why it matters
Chronotype influences when physiological variables are measured relative to biological time, even under the same clock schedule.

Notes
Differences attributed to time of day may partly reflect chronotype if biological timing is not aligned across participants.

Reporting
Interpretation benefits from documenting chronotype or another phase marker in studies involving repeated or time-sensitive measurements.

Related concepts
Circadian rhythm; Phase; DLMO

Circadian rhythm

Unit
Commonly expressed in hours for timing measures

Definition
An approximately 24-hour endogenous rhythm in physiology or behaviour, usually synchronized to environmental time cues.

Why it matters
Circadian timing alters thermoregulation, cardiovascular control, sweating, arousal, and interpretation of many physiological signals.

Notes
Observed daily variation may reflect both endogenous rhythmicity and behavioural or environmental masking.

Reporting
Interpretation should distinguish circadian timing from clock time where possible, especially in studies involving sleep, light, or repeated daily measures.

Related concepts
Phase; Acrophase; Nadir; Entrainment; Chronotype; Time-of-day effect

Cognitive or mental workload

Definition
The demand placed on cognitive processing during task performance or sustained mental effort.

Why it matters
It is relevant because cognitive workload can alter autonomic state, arousal, and interpretation of physiological signals that might otherwise be attributed to thermal exposure.

Notes
Mental workload may affect cardiovascular, electrodermal, and behavioural measures even in thermoneutral conditions.

Related concepts
Arousal; Sympathetic load; Allostatic load

Cross-over design

Definition
A within-subject design in which participants receive multiple conditions or interventions in a defined sequence.

Why it matters
Cross-over designs are efficient for detecting condition differences while controlling for between-person variability.

Notes
They are particularly sensitive to order and carryover effects and therefore require attention to washout, recovery, or adaptation.

Reporting
Reporting should describe sequence allocation, washout provisions, and how period or carryover effects were considered.

Related concepts
Within-subject design; Randomization; Balancing

Cutaneous blood flow

Definition
Blood flow through the skin vasculature contributing to heat exchange between the body and environment.

Why it matters
It is a primary mechanism of dry heat exchange and links vasomotor control to measurable thermal outcomes.

Notes
Often inferred indirectly (e.g. via skin temperature or laser Doppler), not directly measured.

Related concepts
Vasomotor control; Peripheral circulation

D#

Dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO)

Unit
Commonly h

Definition
The time at which melatonin concentration begins to rise under dim-light conditions, used as a marker of circadian phase.

Why it matters
DLMO provides one of the most widely used biological references for aligning physiology to internal time.

Notes
Its estimation depends on assay method, threshold criterion, and environmental light conditions during sampling.

Reporting
Interpretation should state the sampling protocol and threshold used to determine DLMO.

Related concepts
Circadian rhythm; Phase; Entrainment

Distal–proximal gradient (DPG)

Unit
°C

Definition
The temperature difference between distal skin regions, such as the hands or feet, and more proximal regions, such as the torso or thigh.

Why it matters
The gradient provides a sensitive indicator of peripheral vasomotor state and is widely used in studies of thermoregulation, circadian physiology, and sleep onset.

Notes
More positive values are generally associated with distal warming and vasodilation, whereas lower values reflect vasoconstriction. Interpretation depends on the exact sites included in each compartment.

Reporting
Interpretation depends on the anatomical sites included, the averaging procedures applied, and the sign convention used.

Related concepts
Vasomotor control; Vasoconstriction; Circadian rhythm

E#

Effective evaporation

Definition
The portion of sweat secretion that actually evaporates and contributes to heat loss.

Why it matters
Separates thermoeffector activation (sweating) from realised cooling.

Notes
Strongly dependent on humidity, airflow, clothing, and skin wettedness.

Related concepts
Sweating; Thermal load; Heat storage

Effector activation thresholds

Definition
The levels of thermal or related physiological input at which a given thermoeffector response begins to increase above baseline.

Why it matters
Thresholds are central to interpreting when and why sweating, vasodilation, or other responses are recruited.

Notes
Thresholds may vary across individuals and conditions and may shift with circadian timing, sex hormones, fitness, acclimation, or disease.

Reporting
Thresholds should be interpreted with reference to the specific effector, signal, and baseline condition used to define onset.

Related concepts
Interthreshold zone; Thermoeffector; Onset

Energy expenditure (EE)

Unit
Commonly W, kcal·day ⁻¹ , or metabolic equivalents depending on context

Definition
The rate at which energy is used by the body to sustain physiological function and activity.

Why it matters
It is central to thermal physiology because metabolic energy expenditure is a primary source of internal heat production.

Notes
The concept spans resting and active conditions and is often estimated rather than measured directly.

Reporting
Interpretation benefits from distinguishing resting from activity-related expenditure and clarifying whether values are measured, estimated, or inferred.

Related concepts
Calorimetry; Heat-balance equation; Thermal load

Entrainment

Definition
The synchronization of an endogenous rhythm to external time cues such as light–dark cycles, sleep–wake schedules, or social timing.

Why it matters
Entrainment determines how biological timing aligns with the environment and therefore shapes interpretation of repeated physiological measures.

Notes
A rhythm may appear regular yet be weakly or atypically entrained relative to external clock time.

Reporting
Interpretation should distinguish entrainment from phase position and from acute masking by behaviour or environment.

Related concepts
Circadian rhythm; Chronotype; DLMO

F#

Feature extraction

Definition
The derivation of quantitative descriptors from a raw or preprocessed signal, such as peaks, slopes, amplitudes, frequencies, or summary statistics.

Why it matters
Feature extraction is the step that translates a physiological signal into analytical variables used for inference.

Notes
Different extraction choices can produce materially different interpretations even when the underlying signal is the same.

Reporting
Reporting should specify which features are extracted, how they are defined, and what preprocessing precedes them.

Related concepts
Aggregation; Baseline; Onset

Feedback and feed-forward control

Definition
Modes of physiological regulation in which responses are driven either by deviations from a regulated state (feedback) or by anticipatory or predictive cues (feed-forward).

Why it matters
The distinction helps explain why thermoregulatory responses may begin before large internal temperature changes occur or continue to reflect internal state after environmental perturbation.

Notes
Thermoregulation involves both forms of control, and many observed responses represent their interaction rather than a purely reactive mechanism.

Related concepts
Thermoregulation; Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits; Effector activation thresholds

Filtering

Definition
The processing of a signal to attenuate selected frequency components or unwanted variation.

Why it matters
Filtering shapes what remains interpretable in a signal and can materially alter derived features.

Notes
Filters may suppress artefact, baseline drift, or noise, but they may also distort physiological timing, amplitude, or phase relationships.

Reporting
Interpretation should specify filter type, parameters, and purpose rather than referring generically to filtered data.

Related concepts
Smoothing; Noise; Artefact

H#

Heat storage (S)

Unit
Commonly W or W·m ⁻²

Definition
The rate of change in body heat content resulting from an imbalance between heat production and heat loss.

Why it matters
Thermal strain depends on whether heat is accumulating in the body, rather than solely on environmental exposure or perceived comfort.

Notes
Heat storage is a dynamic quantity; similar comfort states may correspond to substantially different levels of accumulated heat. In practice, it may be calculated directly from heat-balance terms or inferred indirectly through changes in core or mean body temperature.

Reporting
Interpretation depends on how heat storage is derived, for example through direct heat-balance calculations or inferred from changes in core or mean body temperature.

Related concepts
Body heat balance; Heat-balance equation; Thermal strain

Heat-balance equation

Unit
Commonly W or W·m ⁻²

Definition
A formal representation of the balance between heat production, heat exchange, and heat storage in the body.

Why it matters
It connects environmental exposure, metabolism, and physiological response within a single interpretive framework.

Notes
Different formulations emphasise different terms, but the central idea is that heat storage reflects the residual of gains and losses.

Reporting
Interpretation benefits from specifying which terms are included, how they are estimated, and whether the equation is used conceptually or quantitatively.

Related concepts
Body heat balance; Heat storage; Thermal load

Hormonal modulation

Definition
Modification of physiological regulation by endocrine signals such as sex hormones, cortisol, thyroid hormones, or melatonin.

Why it matters
Hormonal modulation is important because many thermal, cardiovascular, sudomotor, and circadian responses depend on endocrine context.

Notes
Its effects may alter thresholds, amplitudes, timing, or baseline state rather than simply shifting mean levels.

Related concepts
HPA axis; Circadian rhythm; Acclimation

Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits

Definition
Neural control pathways centred in the hypothalamus that integrate thermal information and coordinate thermoeffector responses.

Why it matters
They provide a mechanistic basis for understanding how body and environmental thermal signals are translated into physiological action.

Notes
These circuits are commonly discussed as central regulators of vasomotion, sweating, shivering, and behavioural responses, although thermoregulation is distributed across broader neural networks.

Related concepts
Thermoregulation; Feedback and feed-forward control; Effector activation thresholds

Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis

Definition
A neuroendocrine system linking hypothalamic and pituitary signaling to adrenal hormone secretion, especially cortisol.

Why it matters
It is relevant because stress-related endocrine modulation can shape arousal, circadian physiology, and thermal or autonomic responses.

Notes
The HPA axis is not a thermal system per se, but it affects physiological state in ways that may modify thermal response and interpretation.

Reporting
Interpretation should distinguish HPA-axis activity from sympathetic activation, as they are related but not identical systems.

Related concepts
Arousal; Allostatic load; Hormonal modulation

I#

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

Definition
Predefined rules determining who or what is eligible to participate in a study and who or what is excluded.

Why it matters
They shape the biological meaning, generalisability, safety, and interpretability of the sample.

Notes
Criteria can unintentionally remove relevant variability or bias the population under study if they are too narrow or poorly justified.

Reporting
Reporting should state the criteria clearly and explain their scientific or practical rationale.

Related concepts
Stratification; Sample size; PICOT

Interindividual variability

Definition
Differences in physiological responses between individuals under similar conditions.

Why it matters
Explains why average responses may not represent any individual.

Notes
Driven by sex, age, body composition, acclimation, circadian timing, and health.

Related concepts
Stratification; Thermal strain

Interthreshold zone

Definition
The range of body temperatures bounded by the activation thresholds for heat-loss and heat-gain effectors, within which overt thermoeffector responses are minimal.

Why it matters
It provides a mechanistic way to think about when sweating, vasodilation, vasoconstriction, or shivering begin, and why those thresholds vary across physiological states.

Notes
Its width and position can shift with circadian timing, sex hormones, fever, acclimation, or ageing.

Reporting
When invoking threshold shifts, it is helpful to specify the effector concerned and the variable used to define onset.

Related concepts
Thermoregulatory set-point; Effector activation thresholds; Thermoneutral zone

L#

Laterality (ipsilateral, contralateral, bilateral, unilateral)

Definition
Descriptors of relation to left/right body sides.

Why it matters
Essential for interpreting asymmetry and comparisons.

Notes
Left/right should be explicit in paired measurements.

Reporting
State whether measurements are unilateral or bilateral.

Related concepts
Spatial orientation; Longitudinal position

Longitudinal position (proximal, distal)

Definition
Descriptors defining position along a limb relative to its origin.

Why it matters
Central to interpreting gradients and site differences.

Notes
Distal regions often show stronger variability than proximal.

Reporting
Include proximal/distal qualifiers where relevant.

Related concepts
Distal–proximal gradient; Spatial orientation

N#

Nadir

Unit
Commonly h

Definition
The lowest point of a rhythmic variable within its cycle.

Why it matters
The nadir is often used to characterise circadian timing in body temperature, alertness, and related variables.

Notes
As with acrophase, the apparent nadir may be influenced by sampling, masking, or irregularity in the signal.

Reporting
Interpretation should clarify how nadir is identified and whether the rhythm is modelled or observed directly.

Related concepts
Circadian rhythm; Phase; Acrophase

Noise

Definition
Unwanted variability in a signal arising from technical, environmental, or non-target physiological sources.

Why it matters
Noise can obscure meaningful physiological patterns and distort derived metrics if not recognised and handled appropriately.

Notes
Not all variability is noise; distinguishing physiological fluctuation from measurement contamination is a core interpretive challenge.

Reporting
Signal-quality judgments should state whether apparent variability is treated as noise, artefact, or meaningful physiological structure.

Related concepts
Artefact; Filtering; Smoothing

Normalization

Definition
A transformation that rescales a signal or feature to improve comparability across units, participants, sessions, or conditions.

Why it matters
Normalisation can reduce irrelevant variance but may also change the substantive interpretation of the result.

Notes
Different normalisation strategies serve different purposes, such as baseline correction, ratio scaling, z-scoring, or within-subject standardisation.

Reporting
Interpretation should state what reference is used for normalisation and what comparability problem it is intended to solve.

Related concepts
Baseline; Aggregation; Feature extraction

O#

Onset

Unit
Commonly s or min, depending on context

Definition
The point at which a physiological response or feature begins to rise above baseline or enters a defined active state.

Why it matters
Onset is often used to interpret thresholds, timing, responsiveness, and sequence of control processes.

Notes
Different operational definitions of onset can yield different conclusions, especially in noisy or slowly changing data.

Reporting
Interpretation should specify the criterion used to define onset, including thresholding, baseline comparison, or model-based detection.

Related concepts
Effector activation thresholds; Baseline; Response time

P#

Parallel design

Definition
A study design in which different participants or groups are assigned to different conditions and remain in those conditions for comparison.

Why it matters
Parallel designs avoid carryover between conditions but may require larger samples because between-person variability remains.

Notes
They are often contrasted with cross-over or within-subject designs.

Reporting
Reporting should state the unit of assignment and whether group comparability was addressed through randomization or balancing.

Related concepts
Between-subject design; Randomization

Peripheral circulation

Definition
Distribution of blood flow to peripheral tissues, especially skin and extremities.

Why it matters
Central to heat exchange and thermoregulatory redistribution.

Related concepts
Cutaneous blood flow; Vascular tone

Peripheral resistance

Definition
The opposition to blood flow offered by the peripheral vasculature.

Why it matters
Changes in peripheral resistance are central to both blood-pressure regulation and thermoregulatory redistribution of blood flow.

Notes
Heat exposure often lowers total peripheral resistance through cutaneous vasodilation, increasing reliance on cardiac adjustments to maintain pressure.

Related concepts
Arterial pressure; Cardiovascular regulation; Vasoconstriction

Phase

Unit
Commonly h or degrees in rhythmic analysis

Definition
The timing of a rhythm relative to a reference point such as clock time, habitual wake time, or another biological marker.

Why it matters
Phase is essential for aligning physiology across individuals and interpreting apparent time-of-day differences.

Notes
Phase may be estimated from different markers, and these are not always interchangeable.

Reporting
Interpretation should specify the phase reference used and whether timing is expressed relative to clock time or biological time.

Related concepts
Circadian rhythm; Acrophase; DLMO

Phasic

Definition
The rapidly changing, event-like, or transient component of a physiological signal.

Why it matters
Phasic structure is often used to interpret momentary responses, discrete activations, or short-latency changes.

Notes
In many signals, the distinction between tonic and phasic components depends partly on the analysis method used to separate them.

Reporting
Interpretation should state how phasic components are identified and what temporal criteria define them.

Related concepts
Tonic; Onset; Feature extraction

Physiological signal

Definition
A measurable time-varying representation of an underlying physiological process.

Why it matters
The distinction between signal and mechanism is central to interpretation, because signals are observed outputs rather than the processes themselves.

Notes
A physiological signal may reflect multiple mechanisms simultaneously and can be shaped by instrumentation, processing, and context.

Reporting
Interpretation should state what underlying process the signal is assumed to index and what limitations apply.

Related concepts
Baseline; Noise; Artefact; Feature extraction

PICOT

Definition
A framework for formulating research questions in terms of Population, Intervention or exposure, Comparator, Outcome, and Time.

Why it matters
It helps clarify study logic and improves the alignment of design, measurement, and interpretation.

Notes
Although developed in clinical research, PICOT can also sharpen environmental and physiological study planning when adapted carefully.

Reporting
Use is most informative when each element is defined explicitly and consistently across the study documentation.

Related concepts
Inclusion/Exclusion criteria; Outcome definition; Sample size

Power

Definition
The probability that a study will detect an effect of a specified size if that effect is truly present.

Why it matters
Power connects sample size, effect size, variability, and decision thresholds in study design.

Notes
It depends not only on participant numbers but also on design structure, repeated measures, signal quality, and modelling choices.

Reporting
Reporting should state the assumptions used in any prospective or retrospective power analysis.

Related concepts
Sample size; Randomization; Within-subject design

Proxy measure

Definition
A measurable variable used to infer an underlying physiological process.

Why it matters
Many physiological signals are indirect representations of mechanisms.

Notes
Interpretation depends on validity of the proxy relationship.

Related concepts
Physiological signal; Artefact

R#

Randomization

Definition
The process of assigning participants, sessions, or conditions by chance according to a predefined rule.

Why it matters
It reduces systematic allocation bias and supports causal inference.

Notes
Randomization may be simple, blocked, stratified, or constrained depending on design needs and sample size.

Reporting
Reporting should state how randomization was implemented and at what level assignment occurred.

Related concepts
Blinding; Block design; Stratification

Recovery

Unit
Commonly s or min

Definition
The return of a physiological variable toward baseline or a new steady state following perturbation.

Why it matters
Recovery carries information about regulation, resilience, and the persistence of thermal or autonomic burden.

Notes
Recovery is not always symmetrical with onset and may involve different mechanisms, timescales, or constraints.

Reporting
Interpretation benefits from defining the recovery interval and the criterion used to judge return or partial return.

Related concepts
Baseline; Response time; Thermal strain

Respiratory sinus arrhythmia

Definition
A rhythmic fluctuation in heart period associated with the respiratory cycle, usually reflecting parasympathetic cardiac modulation.

Why it matters
It is important for interpreting HRV, especially high-frequency components, in thermal and resting studies.

Notes
Respiratory sinus arrhythmia depends on breathing pattern as well as autonomic state, so it cannot be treated as a pure vagal measure without context.

Reporting
Interpretation benefits from explicit consideration of respiration when HRV is used mechanistically.

Related concepts
Heart rate variability; Vagal influences; Baroreflex

Response time

Unit
Commonly s or min

Definition
The delay or time constant with which a measured signal or physiological system responds to a change in input or state.

Why it matters
Response time affects how rapidly a variable reflects thermal or physiological events and therefore shapes apparent agreement between measures.

Notes
The observed response time may reflect both the biology and the sensor or processing pipeline.

Reporting
Interpretation benefits from distinguishing physiological lag from sensor lag and reporting which meaning is intended.

Related concepts
Thermal inertia; Onset; Recovery

S#

Sample size

Definition
The number of participants, observations, or units included in a study or analysis.

Why it matters
Sample size affects precision, power, representativeness, and the ability to model modifiers or interactions.

Notes
The effective sample size may differ from the nominal count depending on clustering, repeated measures, missingness, or exclusion.

Reporting
Reporting should clarify the analysis unit and how the final analysed sample was obtained.

Related concepts
Power; Inclusion/Exclusion criteria; Between-subject design

Sampling rate

Unit
Commonly Hz

Definition
The frequency at which a signal is recorded over time.

Why it matters
Sampling rate constrains temporal resolution, beat detection, spectral interpretation, and comparability across systems.

Notes
An apparently adequate sampling rate for one purpose may be insufficient for another, especially when rapid or fine-scale features are important.

Reporting
Reporting should state the acquisition rate and, where relevant, any downsampling used before analysis.

Related concepts
Response time; Feature extraction; Filtering

Smoothing

Definition
A processing step that reduces short-term variability to emphasize slower trends or more stable patterns.

Why it matters
Smoothing can improve readability and feature estimation but may also obscure rapid physiological events.

Notes
The distinction between smoothing and filtering is often practical rather than absolute, depending on method and intent.

Reporting
Interpretation should state the smoothing approach and the timescale of features that may be attenuated or preserved.

Related concepts
Filtering; Response time; Feature extraction

Spatial orientation (anterior, posterior, medial, lateral, palmar, dorsal)

Definition
Descriptors used to define the position of anatomical sites relative to standard body axes and surfaces.

Why it matters
Clear orientation terminology is essential for reproducible description of measurement sites.

Notes
Palmar and dorsal are region-specific descriptors for hands and feet.

Reporting
Use standard anatomical terms rather than informal descriptors.

Related concepts
Longitudinal position; Laterality

Stationarity

Definition
The assumption that the statistical properties of a signal remain sufficiently constant over the analysis interval.

Why it matters
Many common time-series and HRV methods depend on approximate stationarity for valid interpretation.

Notes
Thermal exposures, posture changes, recovery phases, and transitions can violate stationarity even when signals appear smooth.

Reporting
Interpretation of derived metrics should consider whether the selected window reasonably satisfies the assumptions of the analysis method.

Related concepts
Feature extraction; Baseline; Recovery

Stratification

Definition
The separation of participants or observations into subgroups based on predefined characteristics before allocation or analysis.

Why it matters
Stratification can improve comparability and support interpretation of biologically relevant modifiers.

Notes
Its value depends on the quality and relevance of the stratifying variables and the adequacy of sample size within strata.

Reporting
Reporting should specify the stratification variables, rationale, and whether stratification applied to design, randomization, or analysis.

Related concepts
Randomization; Balancing; Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

Sudomotor activity

Definition
Neural control of sweat gland activation via sympathetic cholinergic pathways.

Why it matters
Represents the control signal for sweating, distinct from measured sweat output.

Related concepts
Sweating; Cholinergic pathways

Sweating

Unit
Commonly g·min ⁻¹ , mg·cm ⁻² ·min ⁻¹ , or latent heat flux units

Definition
The secretion of fluid by eccrine sweat glands onto the skin surface, enabling heat loss through evaporation.

Why it matters
Sweating represents activation of a primary heat-loss effector and contributes to evaporative heat loss when environmental conditions permit evaporation.

Notes
Mechanistically, sweating depends on sympathetic cholinergic activation of eccrine glands. Its interpretation also depends on onset, rate, distribution, and whether secreted sweat actually evaporates rather than accumulating or dripping. Sweat secretion does not itself constitute heat loss. Cooling depends on the fraction of sweat that evaporates rather than accumulates or drips. Interpretation therefore depends on environmental conditions (humidity, air velocity), clothing, and sweat distribution.

Reporting
It is useful to distinguish sweat secretion from effective evaporation and to state whether local, whole-body, or indirect sudomotor measures are being interpreted.

Related concepts
Eccrine glands; Sudomotor activity; Effective evaporation; Autonomic heat-loss drive

Sympathetic and parasympathetic outflow

Definition
Efferent autonomic signaling from central control systems through sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways to peripheral organs and tissues.

Why it matters
These pathways underlie many observed thermoregulatory and cardiovascular adjustments and are frequently inferred from indirect physiological signals.

Notes
The two branches often act in parallel rather than as simple opposites, and their balance varies by organ system and context.

Reporting
Interpretations that invoke sympathetic or parasympathetic outflow should specify the signal used for inference and its physiological limitations.

Related concepts
Autonomic regulation; Vagal influences; Cholinergic pathways

Sympathetic load

Definition
The burden or extent of sympathetic activation imposed by a given physiological or environmental condition.

Why it matters
It helps frame why similar observed responses may differ in cumulative regulatory cost.

Notes
The term is interpretive rather than directly measurable and should not be treated as equivalent to any one signal such as HR, EDA, or LF/HF ratio.

Related concepts
Allostatic load; Arousal; Autonomic regulation

T#

Thermal history

Definition
Prior exposure to thermal conditions influencing current physiological responses.

Why it matters
Affects thresholds, acclimation, and variability.

Related concepts
Acclimation; Thermal strain

Thermal inertia

Definition
The resistance of a body, tissue, or sensor to rapid temperature change due to its thermal mass and transfer properties.

Why it matters
It explains why some measures respond slowly or smooth over rapid transients.

Notes
In thermal physiology, thermal inertia may arise from both biological tissue properties and sensor packaging or placement.

Reporting
Interpretation should consider whether delayed or damped responses reflect physiology, instrumentation, or both.

Related concepts
Response time; Smoothing; Core body temperature

Thermal load

Definition
The thermal challenge imposed on the organism by environmental conditions, activity, clothing, and other factors influencing heat exchange.

Why it matters
The concept helps distinguish external or imposed demand from the physiological response to that demand.

Notes
Thermal load may arise from heat, cold, asymmetry, humidity, radiation, clothing, or activity, and does not by itself specify the magnitude of physiological strain.

Related concepts
Thermal strain; Heat storage; Body heat balance

Thermal steady state

Definition
A condition where body heat storage remains stable over time.

Why it matters
Reference condition for interpreting changes and responses.

Related concepts
Body heat balance; Heat storage

Thermal strain

Definition
The physiological burden imposed on the organism by thermal load, expressed through changes in variables such as core temperature, cardiovascular activity, and sweating.

Why it matters
Thermal-environment studies often prioritise perception, whereas thermal strain reflects the physiological cost associated with maintaining or failing to maintain thermal balance.

Notes
Thermal strain may increase even when subjective discomfort remains modest, particularly during prolonged or repeated exposures. The term therefore captures something different from sensation, preference, or comfort vote, even when these covary.

Related concepts
Heat storage; Thermal load; Body heat balance; Cardiovascular strain

Thermoeffector

Definition
A physiological or behavioural mechanism that alters heat production or heat exchange in response to thermal load.

Why it matters
The term shifts attention from static temperature states to the active mechanisms by which the body regulates those states.

Notes
Sweating, vasodilation, vasoconstriction, shivering, and behavioural changes are all thermoeffectors in different contexts.

Related concepts
Thermoregulation; Autonomic thermoeffector output; Effector activation thresholds

Thermoeffector gain

Definition
The magnitude of effector response per unit change in control signal.

Why it matters
Defines responsiveness of thermoregulatory effectors.

Related concepts
Thermoeffector; Effector activation thresholds

Thermogenesis

Definition
The production of heat within the body through metabolic processes, including both basal cellular activity and regulated physiological mechanisms.

Why it matters
Thermogenesis contributes to maintaining core temperature and overall thermal balance, particularly in cold environments or during metabolic activation.

Notes
Thermogenesis can be classified into shivering thermogenesis (involuntary muscle activity) and non-shivering thermogenesis (primarily mediated by brown adipose tissue and hormonal pathways). It is influenced by factors such as ambient temperature, nutrition, circadian timing, and hormonal state.

Related concepts
Thermoregulation; metabolic rate; brown adipose tissue; shivering; Hormonal modulation

Thermoneutral zone

Definition
The range of environmental conditions within which body temperature can be maintained without substantial increases in metabolic heat production or active evaporative heat loss.

Why it matters
It is a physiological construct and does not necessarily correspond to perceived comfort or preferred indoor environmental conditions.

Notes
Its boundaries depend on clothing, activity, acclimation, and biological factors such as age and sex. In interdisciplinary contexts, the term is often conflated with comfort zone or acceptable indoor range, although these refer to different criteria.

Related concepts
Thermoregulatory set-point; Interthreshold zone; Thermal strain

Thermoregulation

Definition
The coordinated physiological and behavioural processes by which the body regulates heat production and heat exchange to maintain internal temperature within functional limits.

Why it matters
It is the overarching regulatory system linking central control, peripheral effectors, and environmental exchange.

Notes
Thermoregulation includes both autonomic and behavioural components and operates across multiple timescales, from rapid vasomotor responses to longer-term acclimation.

Related concepts
Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits; Thermoeffector; Feedback and feed-forward control; Thermoregulatory control signal

Thermoregulatory control signal

Definition
The central regulatory signal driving thermoeffector activation.

Why it matters
Separates control (drive) from response magnitude.

Related concepts
Thermoeffector; Autonomic heat-loss drive

Thermoregulatory set-point

Definition
The defended value of a regulated thermal variable around which thermoeffector responses are organised.

Why it matters
It helps explain why similar body temperatures may correspond to different regulatory states, such as fever, hormonal modulation, or normal thermoregulation.

Notes
In biological systems, set-points are better understood as functional regulatory references than as fixed universal temperatures.

Related concepts
Interthreshold zone; Thermoregulation; Effector activation thresholds

Time-of-day effect

Definition
Variation in physiological responses depending on time of day.

Why it matters
Affects thermoregulation, cardiovascular responses, and interpretation.

Related concepts
Circadian rhythm; Chronotype

Tonic

Definition
The slowly varying or sustained component of a physiological signal.

Why it matters
The distinction between tonic and phasic structure is important for signals such as EDA, where different timescales carry different interpretations.

Notes
Tonic components are often sensitive to baseline state, drift, or slow regulatory shifts.

Reporting
Interpretation should clarify whether a tonic measure reflects background physiological state, preprocessing structure, or both.

Related concepts
Phasic; Baseline; Filtering

Transient thermal response

Definition
The dynamic physiological response before a new steady state is reached.

Why it matters
Captures short-term adaptation often missed in steady-state analysis.

Related concepts
Thermal steady state; Response time

V#

Vagal influences

Definition
Physiological effects attributable to the vagus nerve, especially parasympathetic modulation of cardiac function.

Why it matters
The concept is important for interpreting HRV, resting heart rate, and cardiovascular adjustment under thermal load.

Notes
Vagal influences are often inferred rather than measured directly and are strongly affected by breathing, posture, sleep, and arousal.

Reporting
Interpretation is improved when the specific surrogate of vagal influence, such as RMSSD or HF power, is made explicit.

Related concepts
Autonomic regulation; Heart rate variability; Respiratory sinus arrhythmia

Vascular tone

Definition
The baseline level of constriction or dilation in blood vessels resulting from active smooth-muscle regulation and local or systemic influences.

Why it matters
It shapes both baseline perfusion and the magnitude of subsequent vasomotor responses.

Notes
Vascular tone is not directly observable in many experiments and is usually inferred from downstream variables such as perfusion, pressure, or temperature.

Related concepts
Vasomotor control; Vasodilation; Vasoconstriction

Vasoconstriction

Definition
A reduction in vessel diameter that decreases local blood flow.

Why it matters
It is a central heat-conservation response and strongly influences distal skin temperature and peripheral perfusion.

Notes
Distal extremities are especially sensitive to vasoconstrictor control, which makes them informative but also potentially misleading if interpreted without context.

Related concepts
Vasomotor control; Distal–proximal gradient; Peripheral resistance

Vasodilation

Definition
An increase in vessel diameter that increases local blood flow; in thermal physiology, this may arise from active neural mechanisms or passive vascular responses.

Why it matters
Vasodilation supports heat dissipation and shapes both skin temperature and cardiovascular demand.

Notes
Active and passive usage varies across literatures, so the intended meaning should be stated clearly when distinctions are important.

Related concepts
Vasomotor control; Vascular tone; Cutaneous vasomotor adjustments

Vasomotor control

Definition
The regulation of blood vessel diameter and associated blood flow distribution, particularly in the skin and peripheral circulation.

Why it matters
It is central to heat exchange, cardiovascular stability, and interpretation of peripheral temperature patterns.

Notes
Vasomotor responses include both vasodilation and vasoconstriction and can be influenced by thermoregulation, posture, arousal, hormones, and disease.

Related concepts
Vasodilation; Vasoconstriction; Vascular tone; Distal–proximal gradient; Cutaneous blood flow

W#

Within-subject design

Definition
A design in which the same participant contributes data under more than one condition.

Why it matters
Within-subject designs can improve statistical efficiency by controlling for stable inter-individual differences.

Notes
They also raise issues of order, carryover, learning, fatigue, or adaptation that require design and analysis attention.

Reporting
Reporting should state condition order, washout or recovery provisions, and how repeated measures were handled analytically.

Related concepts
Cross-over design; Parallel design
