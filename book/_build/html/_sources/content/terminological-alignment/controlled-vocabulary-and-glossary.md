# Controlled vocabulary and glossary

This glossary focuses on physiological measurements and related methodological terminology used in thermal-environment research. It does not attempt to reproduce established built-environment terminology for environmental variables such as air temperature, operative temperature, or relative humidity, which are already well standardized elsewhere. Instead, it concentrates on terms that are used inconsistently across thermal physiology, environmental ergonomics, and measurement practice, especially where site, method, or derivation substantially affect interpretation.

(glossary-a)=
## A

(label-acclimation)=
:::{dropdown} Acclimation

**Definition**  
Physiological or behavioural changes occurring within an organism in response to experimentally induced changes in specific climatic factors.

**Why it matters**  
The term distinguishes responses induced under controlled experimental conditions from responses arising from seasonal or natural environmental exposure.

**Notes**  
In thermal physiology, acclimation is typically treated as a form of phenotypic adaptation to a defined environmental stimulus.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Adaptation <label-adaptation>`; {ref}`Acclimatization <label-acclimatization>`

:::

(label-acclimatization)=
:::{dropdown} Acclimatization

**Definition**  
Physiological or behavioural changes occurring within the lifetime of an organism that reduce strain caused by changes in the natural climate.

**Why it matters**  
The term separates adaptation to natural environmental exposure from adaptation produced by experimental intervention.

**Notes**  
Its historical use is inconsistent, so explicit definition is especially helpful in environmental and occupational studies.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Adaptation <label-adaptation>`; {ref}`Acclimation <label-acclimation>`

:::

(label-acrophase)=
:::{dropdown} Acrophase

**Unit**  
Commonly h

**Definition**  
The timing of the peak of a rhythm within its cycle.

**Why it matters**  
Acrophase is useful for describing when a variable reaches its maximum and for comparing rhythm timing across people or conditions.

**Notes**  
Its estimation depends on sampling density, model choice, and whether the rhythm is sufficiently regular.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation should specify how acrophase is estimated and over what observation period.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Circadian rhythm <label-circadian-rhythm>`; {ref}`Phase <label-phase>`; {ref}`Nadir <label-nadir>`

:::

(label-adaptation)=
:::{dropdown} Adaptation

**Definition**  
Changes that reduce the physiological strain produced by environmental stressors; these changes may be phenotypic or genotypic.

**Why it matters**  
The term is broader than most experimental studies intend, and unqualified use can obscure whether changes are short-term or long-term.

**Notes**  
In thermal physiology, adaptation is often used for reduced strain under repeated or chronic exposure, but its exact meaning benefits from further specification.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Acclimation <label-acclimation>`; {ref}`Acclimatization <label-acclimatization>`

:::

(label-aggregation)=
:::{dropdown} Aggregation

**Definition**  
The combination of multiple values into a summary measure across time, trials, regions, or participants.

**Why it matters**  
Aggregation strongly affects interpretability because it determines the scale at which a phenomenon is represented.

**Notes**  
Averaging may improve stability but can also conceal meaningful within-person, within-region, or temporal variation.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation is strengthened by stating what has been aggregated and what structure may have been lost in the process.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Feature extraction <label-feature-extraction>`; {ref}`Normalisation <label-normalization>`; Mean skin temperature

:::

(label-allostatic-load)=
:::{dropdown} Allostatic load

**Definition**  
The cumulative physiological burden associated with repeated or chronic regulatory effort across interacting systems.

**Why it matters**  
The concept provides a framework for interpreting why chronically demanding thermal or behavioural environments may matter even when short-term comfort is preserved.

**Notes**  
Reduced variability, altered endocrine regulation, and persistent autonomic load may all contribute to allostatic burden, but no single marker is sufficient on its own.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Sympathetic load <label-sympathetic-load>`; {ref}`Arousal <label-arousal>`; {ref}`HPA axis <label-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis>`

:::

(label-arousal)=
:::{dropdown} Arousal

**Definition**  
A state of physiological and psychological activation that can influence autonomic, endocrine, and behavioural responses.

**Why it matters**  
Arousal is important because many physiological signals used in thermal studies are also sensitive to non-thermal activation.

**Notes**  
The term can refer to multiple dimensions, including emotional, cognitive, and physiological activation, which should not be assumed to be identical.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Sympathetic load <label-sympathetic-load>`; {ref}`Cognitive or mental workload <label-cognitive-or-mental-workload>`; {ref}`HPA axis <label-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis>`

:::

(label-artefact)=
:::{dropdown} Artefact

**Definition**  
A distortion or spurious feature in a signal that does not represent the physiological process of interest.

**Why it matters**  
Artefacts can create false structure, bias derived metrics, or be mistaken for meaningful events.

**Notes**  
Artefacts may arise from motion, contact loss, breathing, electrical interference, environmental perturbation, or preprocessing choices.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation is improved when artefact criteria and handling procedures are reported explicitly.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Noise <label-noise>`; {ref}`Filtering <label-filtering>`; {ref}`Stationarity <label-stationarity>`

:::

(label-arterial-pressure)=
:::{dropdown} Arterial pressure

**Unit**  
Commonly mmHg

**Definition**  
The pressure exerted by blood within the arterial system.

**Why it matters**  
Its regulation is essential because thermal and postural challenges can alter vascular resistance and venous return.

**Notes**  
In thermal physiology, arterial pressure is important not only as a cardiovascular endpoint but as a driver of compensatory reflexes such as the baroreflex.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation is improved when pressure is considered alongside flow, resistance, and thermoeffector responses rather than in isolation.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Cardiovascular regulation <label-cardiovascular-regulation>`; {ref}`Baroreflex <label-baroreflex>`; {ref}`Peripheral resistance <label-peripheral-resistance>`

:::

(label-arteriovenous-anastomoses)=
:::{dropdown} Arteriovenous anastomoses (AVA)

**Definition**  
Specialized vascular shunts that allow blood to bypass capillary beds, especially in acral regions such as the hands and feet.

**Why it matters**  
They play a major role in distal heat exchange and therefore help explain strong thermal responses at peripheral sites.

**Notes**  
AVA-rich regions are especially important in studies of distal warming, vasoconstriction, and sleep-related thermoregulation.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation should distinguish AVA-rich glabrous sites from non-glabrous skin, as they do not behave identically.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Vasomotor control <label-vasomotor-control>`; {ref}`Distal–proximal gradient <label-distal-proximal-gradient>`; Skin blood flow

:::

(label-autonomic-heat-loss-drive)=
:::{dropdown} Autonomic heat-loss drive

**Definition**  
The autonomic regulatory tendency to promote heat dissipation through mechanisms such as sweating and cutaneous vasodilation.

**Why it matters**  
The concept is useful for distinguishing the control signal for heat loss from the observable magnitude of the resulting response.

**Notes**  
Heat-loss drive may be present even when measured heat loss is constrained by environment, clothing, or local measurement limitations.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Thermoeffector <label-thermoeffector>`; {ref}`Sweating <label-sweating>`; {ref}`Vasodilation <label-vasodilation>`

:::

(label-autonomic-regulation)=
:::{dropdown} Autonomic regulation

**Definition**  
The regulation of physiological function through the autonomic nervous system, including sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways.

**Why it matters**  
It links thermal, cardiovascular, sudomotor, and neuroendocrine responses that are often measured separately in experiments.

**Notes**  
In thermal physiology, autonomic regulation underlies sweating, vasomotor adjustments, cardiac responses, and aspects of circadian and stress-related modulation.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Sympathetic and parasympathetic outflow <label-sympathetic-and-parasympathetic-outflow>`; {ref}`Vagal influences <label-vagal-influences>`; {ref}`Central autonomic regulation <label-central-autonomic-regulation>`

:::

(label-autonomic-thermoeffector-output)=
:::{dropdown} Autonomic thermoeffector output

**Definition**  
The autonomically mediated physiological output of thermoregulatory effectors such as sweating, vasomotion, or cardiovascular adjustment.

**Why it matters**  
It links central regulation to observable peripheral responses measured in experiments.

**Notes**  
The phrase is useful when discussing thermoeffector activity at the system level rather than the specific mechanism of a single response.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Thermoeffector <label-thermoeffector>`; {ref}`Autonomic regulation <label-autonomic-regulation>`; {ref}`Sweating <label-sweating>`

:::

(glossary-b)=
## B

(label-balancing)=
:::{dropdown} Balancing

**Definition**  
The design practice of distributing conditions or participant characteristics so that groups or sequences are as comparable as possible.

**Why it matters**  
Balancing reduces the risk that systematic asymmetry drives apparent effects.

**Notes**  
Balancing may be achieved through randomization, blocking, stratification, or sequence design rather than through a single method.

**Reporting**  
Reporting should state what was balanced and by what mechanism.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Randomization <label-randomization>`; {ref}`Block design <label-block-design>`; {ref}`Stratification <label-stratification>`

:::

(label-baroreflex)=
:::{dropdown} Baroreflex

**Definition**  
A short-latency cardiovascular feedback mechanism that adjusts heart rate and vascular tone in response to changes in arterial pressure.

**Why it matters**  
It is important because thermal stress changes skin blood flow and venous return, thereby altering the cardiovascular control problem that must be stabilised.

**Notes**  
Baroreflex-related influences are one reason HRV changes under heat cannot be interpreted solely as changes in sympathetic dominance.

**Reporting**  
The term is most informative when a feedback-control interpretation is intended and distinguished from generic autonomic activation.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Arterial pressure <label-arterial-pressure>`; Heart rate variability; {ref}`Cardiovascular regulation <label-cardiovascular-regulation>`

:::

(label-baseline)=
:::{dropdown} Baseline

**Definition**  
A reference level of a signal or variable measured before a perturbation, event, or analysis interval of interest.

**Why it matters**  
Baseline provides the contextual starting point from which change, onset, recovery, or reactivity are interpreted.

**Notes**  
Baseline values are not neutral by default; they depend on posture, timing, context, prior exposure, and preprocessing choices.

**Reporting**  
It is helpful to define the duration, timing, and conditions used to establish baseline values.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Onset <label-onset>`; {ref}`Recovery <label-recovery>`; {ref}`Normalisation <label-normalization>`; {ref}`Thermal steady state <label-thermal-steady-state>`; {ref}`Transient thermal response <label-transient-thermal-response>`

:::

(label-between-subject-design)=
:::{dropdown} Between-subject design

**Definition**  
A design in which comparisons are made across different participants allocated to different conditions.

**Why it matters**  
It avoids within-person carryover but is more affected by inter-individual variability than repeated-measures designs.

**Notes**  
Between-subject comparisons depend heavily on allocation quality, group comparability, and adequate sample size.

**Reporting**  
Reporting should describe assignment, comparability procedures, and handling of baseline differences.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Parallel design <label-parallel-design>`; {ref}`Randomization <label-randomization>`; {ref}`Blinding <label-blinding>`

:::

(label-blinding)=
:::{dropdown} Blinding

**Definition**  
A design feature in which participants, investigators, analysts, or some combination are kept unaware of condition assignment or study hypotheses.

**Why it matters**  
Blinding reduces bias arising from expectation, differential treatment, or selective interpretation.

**Notes**  
In environmental and physiological studies, full blinding may be difficult when exposures are perceptible, but partial blinding may still be possible.

**Reporting**  
Reporting should specify who was blinded, to what information, and how blinding was maintained or limited.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Randomization <label-randomization>`; {ref}`Between-subject design <label-between-subject-design>`

:::

(label-block-design)=
:::{dropdown} Block design

**Definition**  
A design or allocation strategy in which conditions or assignments are organized into blocks to improve balance or control nuisance variation.

**Why it matters**  
Blocking can reduce confounding by time, order, batch, or participant characteristics.

**Notes**  
The term may refer to experimental structure or randomization strategy, so its meaning should be specified.

**Reporting**  
Reporting should clarify what is being blocked and why.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Randomization <label-randomization>`; {ref}`Balancing <label-balancing>`; {ref}`Stratification <label-stratification>`

:::

(label-body-heat-balance)=
:::{dropdown} Body heat balance

**Definition**  
The condition in which total heat gain equals total heat loss, such that no net body heat is stored.

**Why it matters**  
It provides the conceptual basis for interpreting whether the body is thermally stable, compensating, or accumulating heat.

**Notes**  
A state of body heat balance may be achieved with very different physiological costs, including sweating, vasodilation, or cardiovascular strain.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Heat-balance equation <label-heat-balance-equation>`; {ref}`Heat storage <label-heat-storage>`; {ref}`Thermal load <label-thermal-load>`; {ref}`Thermal strain <label-thermal-strain>`

:::

(glossary-c)=
## C

(label-calorimetry)=
:::{dropdown} Calorimetry (direct and indirect)

**Definition**  
A family of methods used to estimate energy expenditure or heat exchange, either by measuring heat directly or by inferring it from gas exchange.

**Why it matters**  
It provides the methodological bridge between metabolism and thermal interpretation.

**Notes**  
Direct calorimetry refers to direct measurement of heat transfer, whereas indirect calorimetry estimates energy expenditure from oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation should state whether calorimetry is direct or indirect and which derived variables are used in subsequent analysis.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Energy expenditure <label-energy-expenditure>`; {ref}`Heat-balance equation <label-heat-balance-equation>`; Resting energy expenditure

:::

(label-cardiac-output)=
:::{dropdown} Cardiac output (CO)

**Unit**  
Commonly L·min ⁻¹

**Definition**  
The volume of blood pumped by the heart per unit time.

**Why it matters**  
It is a central determinant of convective heat transport and perfusion during thermal stress.

**Notes**  
In heat exposure, cardiac output often increases to support both arterial pressure and cutaneous heat transfer. Important not to confuse with Stroke Volume; the amount of blood volume pumped out of the heart per beat, calculated as the difference between the end-diastolic volume and the end-systolic volume.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation benefits from clarifying whether cardiac output is measured directly, estimated, or inferred from related cardiovascular variables.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Cardiovascular regulation <label-cardiovascular-regulation>`; {ref}`Chronotropic effort <label-chronotropic-effort>`; {ref}`Peripheral resistance <label-peripheral-resistance>`

:::

(label-cardiovascular-regulation)=
:::{dropdown} Cardiovascular regulation

**Definition**  
The integrated control of heart function, vascular resistance, pressure, and flow distribution needed to maintain perfusion and support thermal adjustment.

**Why it matters**  
It links thermoregulatory demands to circulatory effort and therefore helps explain why similar temperatures may impose different physiological costs.

**Notes**  
In thermal physiology, cardiovascular regulation often includes changes in heart rate, cardiac output, peripheral resistance, and pressure control.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Cardiac output <label-cardiac-output>`; {ref}`Arterial pressure <label-arterial-pressure>`; {ref}`Peripheral resistance <label-peripheral-resistance>`; {ref}`Baroreflex <label-baroreflex>`

:::

(label-cardiovascular-strain)=
:::{dropdown} Cardiovascular strain

**Definition**  
The physiological burden placed on the cardiovascular system in response to thermal, environmental, or regulatory demand.

**Why it matters**  
It distinguishes between observed cardiovascular responses and the underlying effort required to maintain perfusion and support thermoregulation.

**Notes**  
Cardiovascular strain may increase even when core temperature or pressure remain stable, reflecting compensatory effort rather than failure.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Cardiovascular workload <label-cardiovascular-workload>`; {ref}`Thermal strain <label-thermal-strain>`; {ref}`Allostatic load <label-allostatic-load>`

:::

(label-cardiovascular-workload)=
:::{dropdown} Cardiovascular workload

**Definition**  
The circulatory effort required to maintain perfusion and support physiological demands, including thermoregulation.

**Why it matters**  
It provides an interpretation layer for why thermal states with similar temperatures may differ in cardiovascular cost.

**Notes**  
The term is broader than heart rate alone and may reflect the combined demands placed on cardiac output, pressure regulation, and vascular control.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Cardiac output <label-cardiac-output>`; {ref}`Chronotropic effort <label-chronotropic-effort>`; {ref}`Thermal strain <label-thermal-strain>`

:::

(label-central-autonomic-regulation)=
:::{dropdown} Central autonomic regulation

**Definition**  
The central neural coordination of autonomic responses across cardiovascular, thermoregulatory, sudomotor, and neuroendocrine systems.

**Why it matters**  
It provides a bridge between central regulation and peripheral physiological outputs.

**Notes**  
The term is broader than any single pathway and is useful when multiple autonomic outputs are considered together.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Autonomic regulation <label-autonomic-regulation>`; {ref}`Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits <label-hypothalamic-thermoregulatory-circuits>`; {ref}`Allostatic load <label-allostatic-load>`

:::

(label-cholinergic-pathways)=
:::{dropdown} Cholinergic pathways

**Definition**  
Neural pathways in which acetylcholine acts as the primary neurotransmitter.

**Why it matters**  
They are especially relevant in thermal physiology because sympathetic cholinergic activation drives eccrine sweating.

**Notes**  
The term should not be equated simplistically with parasympathetic function, since sympathetic cholinergic pathways are a key exception.

**Reporting**  
Where cholinergic mechanisms are invoked, it is helpful to specify whether the context is autonomic, sudomotor, or broader neurophysiological control.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Autonomic regulation <label-autonomic-regulation>`; {ref}`Sweating <label-sweating>`; Eccrine glands

:::

(label-chronotropic-effort)=
:::{dropdown} Chronotropic effort

**Definition**  
The cardiac effort associated specifically with adjustment of heart rate.

**Why it matters**  
The term is useful when thermal or circulatory demand is met primarily through heart-rate change rather than through stroke-volume adjustment.

**Notes**  
In passive heat exposure, increased chronotropic effort often reflects the cardiovascular cost of supporting heat dissipation while maintaining pressure.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Cardiovascular workload <label-cardiovascular-workload>`; Heart rate variability; {ref}`Cardiac output <label-cardiac-output>`

:::

(label-chronotype)=
:::{dropdown} Chronotype

**Definition**  
A characteristic pattern of preferred or habitual timing of sleep, activity, and associated circadian phase.

**Why it matters**  
Chronotype influences when physiological variables are measured relative to biological time, even under the same clock schedule.

**Notes**  
Differences attributed to time of day may partly reflect chronotype if biological timing is not aligned across participants.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation benefits from documenting chronotype or another phase marker in studies involving repeated or time-sensitive measurements.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Circadian rhythm <label-circadian-rhythm>`; {ref}`Phase <label-phase>`; {ref}`DLMO <label-dim-light-melatonin-onset>`

:::

(label-circadian-rhythm)=
:::{dropdown} Circadian rhythm

**Unit**  
Commonly expressed in hours for timing measures

**Definition**  
An approximately 24-hour endogenous rhythm in physiology or behaviour, usually synchronized to environmental time cues.

**Why it matters**  
Circadian timing alters thermoregulation, cardiovascular control, sweating, arousal, and interpretation of many physiological signals.

**Notes**  
Observed daily variation may reflect both endogenous rhythmicity and behavioural or environmental masking.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation should distinguish circadian timing from clock time where possible, especially in studies involving sleep, light, or repeated daily measures.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Phase <label-phase>`; {ref}`Acrophase <label-acrophase>`; {ref}`Nadir <label-nadir>`; {ref}`Entrainment <label-entrainment>`; {ref}`Chronotype <label-chronotype>`; {ref}`Time-of-day effect <label-time-of-day-effect>`

:::

(label-cognitive-or-mental-workload)=
:::{dropdown} Cognitive or mental workload

**Definition**  
The demand placed on cognitive processing during task performance or sustained mental effort.

**Why it matters**  
It is relevant because cognitive workload can alter autonomic state, arousal, and interpretation of physiological signals that might otherwise be attributed to thermal exposure.

**Notes**  
Mental workload may affect cardiovascular, electrodermal, and behavioural measures even in thermoneutral conditions.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Arousal <label-arousal>`; {ref}`Sympathetic load <label-sympathetic-load>`; {ref}`Allostatic load <label-allostatic-load>`

:::

(label-cross-over-design)=
:::{dropdown} Cross-over design

**Definition**  
A within-subject design in which participants receive multiple conditions or interventions in a defined sequence.

**Why it matters**  
Cross-over designs are efficient for detecting condition differences while controlling for between-person variability.

**Notes**  
They are particularly sensitive to order and carryover effects and therefore require attention to washout, recovery, or adaptation.

**Reporting**  
Reporting should describe sequence allocation, washout provisions, and how period or carryover effects were considered.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Within-subject design <label-within-subject-design>`; {ref}`Randomization <label-randomization>`; {ref}`Balancing <label-balancing>`

:::

(label-cutaneous-blood-flow)=
:::{dropdown} Cutaneous blood flow

**Definition**  
Blood flow through the skin vasculature contributing to heat exchange between the body and environment.

**Why it matters**  
It is a primary mechanism of dry heat exchange and links vasomotor control to measurable thermal outcomes.

**Notes**  
Often inferred indirectly (e.g. via skin temperature or laser Doppler), not directly measured.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Vasomotor control <label-vasomotor-control>`; {ref}`Peripheral circulation <label-peripheral-circulation>`

:::

(glossary-d)=
## D

(label-dim-light-melatonin-onset)=
:::{dropdown} Dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO)

**Unit**  
Commonly h

**Definition**  
The time at which melatonin concentration begins to rise under dim-light conditions, used as a marker of circadian phase.

**Why it matters**  
DLMO provides one of the most widely used biological references for aligning physiology to internal time.

**Notes**  
Its estimation depends on assay method, threshold criterion, and environmental light conditions during sampling.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation should state the sampling protocol and threshold used to determine DLMO.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Circadian rhythm <label-circadian-rhythm>`; {ref}`Phase <label-phase>`; {ref}`Entrainment <label-entrainment>`

:::

(label-distal-proximal-gradient)=
:::{dropdown} Distal–proximal gradient (DPG)

**Unit**  
°C

**Definition**  
The temperature difference between distal skin regions, such as the hands or feet, and more proximal regions, such as the torso or thigh.

**Why it matters**  
The gradient provides a sensitive indicator of peripheral vasomotor state and is widely used in studies of thermoregulation, circadian physiology, and sleep onset.

**Notes**  
More positive values are generally associated with distal warming and vasodilation, whereas lower values reflect vasoconstriction. Interpretation depends on the exact sites included in each compartment.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation depends on the anatomical sites included, the averaging procedures applied, and the sign convention used.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Vasomotor control <label-vasomotor-control>`; {ref}`Vasoconstriction <label-vasoconstriction>`; {ref}`Circadian rhythm <label-circadian-rhythm>`

:::

(glossary-e)=
## E

(label-effective-evaporation)=
:::{dropdown} Effective evaporation

**Definition**  
The portion of sweat secretion that actually evaporates and contributes to heat loss.

**Why it matters**  
Separates thermoeffector activation (sweating) from realised cooling.

**Notes**  
Strongly dependent on humidity, airflow, clothing, and skin wettedness.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Sweating <label-sweating>`; {ref}`Thermal load <label-thermal-load>`; {ref}`Heat storage <label-heat-storage>`

:::

(label-effector-activation-thresholds)=
:::{dropdown} Effector activation thresholds

**Definition**  
The levels of thermal or related physiological input at which a given thermoeffector response begins to increase above baseline.

**Why it matters**  
Thresholds are central to interpreting when and why sweating, vasodilation, or other responses are recruited.

**Notes**  
Thresholds may vary across individuals and conditions and may shift with circadian timing, sex hormones, fitness, acclimation, or disease.

**Reporting**  
Thresholds should be interpreted with reference to the specific effector, signal, and baseline condition used to define onset.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Interthreshold zone <label-interthreshold-zone>`; {ref}`Thermoeffector <label-thermoeffector>`; {ref}`Onset <label-onset>`

:::

(label-energy-expenditure)=
:::{dropdown} Energy expenditure (EE)

**Unit**  
Commonly W, kcal·day ⁻¹ , or metabolic equivalents depending on context

**Definition**  
The rate at which energy is used by the body to sustain physiological function and activity.

**Why it matters**  
It is central to thermal physiology because metabolic energy expenditure is a primary source of internal heat production.

**Notes**  
The concept spans resting and active conditions and is often estimated rather than measured directly.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation benefits from distinguishing resting from activity-related expenditure and clarifying whether values are measured, estimated, or inferred.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Calorimetry <label-calorimetry>`; {ref}`Heat-balance equation <label-heat-balance-equation>`; {ref}`Thermal load <label-thermal-load>`

:::

(label-entrainment)=
:::{dropdown} Entrainment

**Definition**  
The synchronization of an endogenous rhythm to external time cues such as light–dark cycles, sleep–wake schedules, or social timing.

**Why it matters**  
Entrainment determines how biological timing aligns with the environment and therefore shapes interpretation of repeated physiological measures.

**Notes**  
A rhythm may appear regular yet be weakly or atypically entrained relative to external clock time.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation should distinguish entrainment from phase position and from acute masking by behaviour or environment.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Circadian rhythm <label-circadian-rhythm>`; {ref}`Chronotype <label-chronotype>`; {ref}`DLMO <label-dim-light-melatonin-onset>`

:::

(glossary-f)=
## F

(label-feature-extraction)=
:::{dropdown} Feature extraction

**Definition**  
The derivation of quantitative descriptors from a raw or preprocessed signal, such as peaks, slopes, amplitudes, frequencies, or summary statistics.

**Why it matters**  
Feature extraction is the step that translates a physiological signal into analytical variables used for inference.

**Notes**  
Different extraction choices can produce materially different interpretations even when the underlying signal is the same.

**Reporting**  
Reporting should specify which features are extracted, how they are defined, and what preprocessing precedes them.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Aggregation <label-aggregation>`; {ref}`Baseline <label-baseline>`; {ref}`Onset <label-onset>`

:::

(label-feedback-and-feed-forward-control)=
:::{dropdown} Feedback and feed-forward control

**Definition**  
Modes of physiological regulation in which responses are driven either by deviations from a regulated state (feedback) or by anticipatory or predictive cues (feed-forward).

**Why it matters**  
The distinction helps explain why thermoregulatory responses may begin before large internal temperature changes occur or continue to reflect internal state after environmental perturbation.

**Notes**  
Thermoregulation involves both forms of control, and many observed responses represent their interaction rather than a purely reactive mechanism.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Thermoregulation <label-thermoregulation>`; {ref}`Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits <label-hypothalamic-thermoregulatory-circuits>`; {ref}`Effector activation thresholds <label-effector-activation-thresholds>`

:::

(label-filtering)=
:::{dropdown} Filtering

**Definition**  
The processing of a signal to attenuate selected frequency components or unwanted variation.

**Why it matters**  
Filtering shapes what remains interpretable in a signal and can materially alter derived features.

**Notes**  
Filters may suppress artefact, baseline drift, or noise, but they may also distort physiological timing, amplitude, or phase relationships.

**Reporting**

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Thermoeffector <label-thermoeffector>`; {ref}`Effector activation thresholds <label-effector-activation-thresholds>`

:::

(label-thermogenesis)=
:::{dropdown} Thermogenesis

**Definition**  
The production of heat within the body through metabolic processes, including both basal cellular activity and regulated physiological mechanisms.

**Why it matters**  
Thermogenesis contributes to maintaining core temperature and overall thermal balance, particularly in cold environments or during metabolic activation.

**Notes**  
Thermogenesis can be classified into shivering thermogenesis (involuntary muscle activity) and non-shivering thermogenesis (primarily mediated by brown adipose tissue and hormonal pathways). It is influenced by factors such as ambient temperature, nutrition, circadian timing, and hormonal state.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`thermoregulation`; {ref}`metabolic-rate`; {ref}`brown-adipose-tissue`; {ref}`shivering`; {ref}`hormonal-modulation`

:::

(label-thermoneutral-zone)=
:::{dropdown} Thermoneutral zone

**Definition**  
The range of environmental conditions within which body temperature can be maintained without substantial increases in metabolic heat production or active evaporative heat loss.

**Why it matters**  
It is a physiological construct and does not necessarily correspond to perceived comfort or preferred indoor environmental conditions.

**Notes**  
Its boundaries depend on clothing, activity, acclimation, and biological factors such as age and sex. In interdisciplinary contexts, the term is often conflated with comfort zone or acceptable indoor range, although these refer to different criteria.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Thermoregulatory set-point <label-thermoregulatory-set-point>`; {ref}`Interthreshold zone <label-interthreshold-zone>`; {ref}`Thermal strain <label-thermal-strain>`

:::

(label-thermoregulation)=
:::{dropdown} Thermoregulation

**Definition**  
The coordinated physiological and behavioural processes by which the body regulates heat production and heat exchange to maintain internal temperature within functional limits.

**Why it matters**  
It is the overarching regulatory system linking central control, peripheral effectors, and environmental exchange.

**Notes**  
Thermoregulation includes both autonomic and behavioural components and operates across multiple timescales, from rapid vasomotor responses to longer-term acclimation.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits <label-hypothalamic-thermoregulatory-circuits>`; {ref}`Thermoeffector <label-thermoeffector>`; {ref}`Feedback and feed-forward control <label-feedback-and-feed-forward-control>`; {ref}`Thermoregulatory control signal <label-thermoregulatory-control-signal>`

:::

(label-thermoregulatory-control-signal)=
:::{dropdown} Thermoregulatory control signal

**Definition**  
The central regulatory signal driving thermoeffector activation.

**Why it matters**  
Separates control (drive) from response magnitude.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Thermoeffector <label-thermoeffector>`; {ref}`Autonomic heat-loss drive <label-autonomic-heat-loss-drive>`

:::

(label-thermoregulatory-set-point)=
:::{dropdown} Thermoregulatory set-point

**Definition**  
The defended value of a regulated thermal variable around which thermoeffector responses are organised.

**Why it matters**  
It helps explain why similar body temperatures may correspond to different regulatory states, such as fever, hormonal modulation, or normal thermoregulation.

**Notes**  
In biological systems, set-points are better understood as functional regulatory references than as fixed universal temperatures.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Interthreshold zone <label-interthreshold-zone>`; {ref}`Thermoregulation <label-thermoregulation>`; {ref}`Effector activation thresholds <label-effector-activation-thresholds>`

:::

(label-time-of-day-effect)=
:::{dropdown} Time-of-day effect

**Definition**  
Variation in physiological responses depending on time of day.

**Why it matters**  
Affects thermoregulation, cardiovascular responses, and interpretation.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Circadian rhythm <label-circadian-rhythm>`; {ref}`Chronotype <label-chronotype>`

:::

(label-tonic)=
:::{dropdown} Tonic

**Definition**  
The slowly varying or sustained component of a physiological signal.

**Why it matters**  
The distinction between tonic and phasic structure is important for signals such as EDA, where different timescales carry different interpretations.

**Notes**  
Tonic components are often sensitive to baseline state, drift, or slow regulatory shifts.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation should clarify whether a tonic measure reflects background physiological state, preprocessing structure, or both.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Phasic <label-phasic>`; {ref}`Baseline <label-baseline>`; {ref}`Filtering <label-filtering>`

:::

(label-transient-thermal-response)=
:::{dropdown} Transient thermal response

**Definition**  
The dynamic physiological response before a new steady state is reached.

**Why it matters**  
Captures short-term adaptation often missed in steady-state analysis.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Thermal steady state <label-thermal-steady-state>`; {ref}`Response time <label-response-time>`

(glossary-v)=
## V

(label-vagal-influences)=
:::{dropdown} Vagal influences

**Definition**  
Physiological effects attributable to the vagus nerve, especially parasympathetic modulation of cardiac function.

**Why it matters**  
The concept is important for interpreting HRV, resting heart rate, and cardiovascular adjustment under thermal load.

**Notes**  
Vagal influences are often inferred rather than measured directly and are strongly affected by breathing, posture, sleep, and arousal.

**Reporting**  
Interpretation is improved when the specific surrogate of vagal influence, such as RMSSD or HF power, is made explicit.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Autonomic regulation <label-autonomic-regulation>`; Heart rate variability; {ref}`Respiratory sinus arrhythmia <label-respiratory-sinus-arrhythmia>`

:::

(label-vascular-tone)=
:::{dropdown} Vascular tone

**Definition**  
The baseline level of constriction or dilation in blood vessels resulting from active smooth-muscle regulation and local or systemic influences.

**Why it matters**  
It shapes both baseline perfusion and the magnitude of subsequent vasomotor responses.

**Notes**  
Vascular tone is not directly observable in many experiments and is usually inferred from downstream variables such as perfusion, pressure, or temperature.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Vasomotor control <label-vasomotor-control>`; {ref}`Vasodilation <label-vasodilation>`; {ref}`Vasoconstriction <label-vasoconstriction>`

:::

(label-vasoconstriction)=
:::{dropdown} Vasoconstriction

**Definition**  
A reduction in vessel diameter that decreases local blood flow.

**Why it matters**  
It is a central heat-conservation response and strongly influences distal skin temperature and peripheral perfusion.

**Notes**  
Distal extremities are especially sensitive to vasoconstrictor control, which makes them informative but also potentially misleading if interpreted without context.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Vasomotor control <label-vasomotor-control>`; {ref}`Distal–proximal gradient <label-distal-proximal-gradient>`; {ref}`Peripheral resistance <label-peripheral-resistance>`

:::

(label-vasodilation)=
:::{dropdown} Vasodilation (active and passive)

**Definition**  
An increase in vessel diameter that increases local blood flow; in thermal physiology, this may arise from active neural mechanisms or passive vascular responses.

**Why it matters**  
Vasodilation supports heat dissipation and shapes both skin temperature and cardiovascular demand.

**Notes**  
Active and passive usage varies across literatures, so the intended meaning should be stated clearly when distinctions are important.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Vasomotor control <label-vasomotor-control>`; {ref}`Vascular tone <label-vascular-tone>`; Cutaneous vasomotor adjustments

:::

(label-vasomotor-control)=
:::{dropdown} Vasomotor control

**Definition**  
The regulation of blood vessel diameter and associated blood flow distribution, particularly in the skin and peripheral circulation.

**Why it matters**  
It is central to heat exchange, cardiovascular stability, and interpretation of peripheral temperature patterns.

**Notes**  
Vasomotor responses include both vasodilation and vasoconstriction and can be influenced by thermoregulation, posture, arousal, hormones, and disease.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Vasodilation <label-vasodilation>`; {ref}`Vasoconstriction <label-vasoconstriction>`; {ref}`Vascular tone <label-vascular-tone>`; {ref}`Distal–proximal gradient <label-distal-proximal-gradient>`; {ref}`Cutaneous blood flow <label-cutaneous-blood-flow>`

:::

## W

(label-within-subject-design)=
:::{dropdown} Within-subject design

**Definition**  
A design in which the same participant contributes data under more than one condition.

**Why it matters**  
Within-subject designs can improve statistical efficiency by controlling for stable inter-individual differences.

**Notes**  
They also raise issues of order, carryover, learning, fatigue, or adaptation that require design and analysis attention.

**Reporting**  
Reporting should state condition order, washout or recovery provisions, and how repeated measures were handled analytically.

**Related concepts**  
{ref}`Cross-over design <label-cross-over-design>`; {ref}`Parallel design <label-parallel-design>`
