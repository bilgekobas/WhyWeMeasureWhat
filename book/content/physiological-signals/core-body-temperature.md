# Core body temperature

### Why: the mechanistic reasons behind the measurement

Core body temperature (CBT) represents the most direct indicator of whole-body {ref}`heat storage <label-heat-storage>` and internal {ref}`thermal strain <label-thermal-strain>`. It integrates metabolic {ref}`heat production <label-thermogenesis>` with convective, radiative, evaporative, and conductive losses, reflecting the net balance of the body's thermal budget {cite:p}`werner_system_2010`. Even small CBT deviations can alter cardiovascular efficiency, neural conductivity, and endocrine signalling {cite:p}`taylor_human_2014`.

Unlike skin temperature, which tracks environmental exchanges, CBT is actively defended near 36.5--37.5 °C in healthy adults through {ref}`hypothalamic thermoregulatory circuits <label-hypothalamic-thermoregulatory-circuits>` combining {ref}`feedback and feed-forward control <label-feedback-and-feed-forward-control>` of {ref}`vasomotion <label-vasomotor-control>`, {ref}`sweating <label-sweating>`, and metabolic heat production {cite:p}`romanovsky_thermoregulation_2007,tan_regulation_2018`. During passive heat exposure, CBT increases linearly with cumulative {ref}`heat storage <label-heat-storage>` and blood redistribution, serving as the primary input for {ref}`effector activation thresholds <label-effector-activation-thresholds>`.

Because subjective thermal comfort or sensation can diverge from physiological strain, continuous CBT monitoring provides a quantitative index of {ref}`thermal load <label-thermal-load>`, safety limits, and {ref}`adaptation <label-adaptation>` potential. Sustained CBT elevations ≥ 38.0--38.5 °C denote excessive physiological strain in occupational and environmental contexts {cite:p}`noauthor_iso_2004`, whereas levels ≥ 40.0--40.5 °C accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction define exertional heat stroke {cite:p}`armstrong_exertional_2007`.

### How: sensor types for measurement 

A variety of sensors and techniques are used to estimate CBT, differing in anatomical site, thermal {ref}`response time <label-response-time>`, invasiveness, and susceptibility to environmental error. Sensor types include thermal probes and thermocouples (measuring conductive heat transfer), infrared thermometers (radiative detection), thermistors with telemetry capability, and heat-flux capacitors (inferring deep temperature from surface gradients).

### Where: body sites of measurement

**Rectal thermometry.** Flexible thermistor probes inserted approximately 10--15 cm beyond the anal sphincter remain a laboratory reference. Insertion to near 15 cm minimises local gradient error and improves reproducibility {cite:p}`miller_validity_2017`. Rectal probes typically respond within 10--30 s and exhibit an accuracy of ±0.1 °C under quasi-steady conditions {cite:p}`hymczak_core_2021`. Because of the surrounding tissue's {ref}`thermal inertia <label-thermal-inertia>`, rectal temperature lags rapid internal shifts by 5--15 min, providing a time-integrated estimate of core heat content {cite:p}`greenes_when_2004`.

**Oesophageal thermometry.** A lubricated thermistor or thermocouple is advanced \~35--45 cm through the nostril into the lower oesophagus, aligning with central blood temperature {cite:p}`mekjavic_determination_1990`. Because of its proximity to large vessels, the oesophageal probe captures rapid changes in core blood temperature within \~10 s {cite:p}`lee_core_2000`, and leads rectal measurements during both heating and cooling phases. Tolerance issues, probe displacement, and discomfort limit its use to short-term laboratory protocols.

**Gastrointestinal telemetry pills.** Ingestible capsules containing a precision thermistor and radio transmitter provide wireless measurements at 1--4 Hz sampling. Under controlled conditions, accuracy is ±0.1 °C {cite:p}`obrien_telemetry_1998`. Transit time through the stomach and small intestine varies between 12 and 72 h, and measurements become invalid once the capsule enters the colon, typically marked by an abrupt temperature drop {cite:p}`teunissen_telemetry_2012`. Telemetry pills display intermediate dynamics, slower than oesophageal but faster than rectal responses {cite:p}`obrien_telemetry_1998`.

**Tympanic thermometry.** Infrared tympanic devices estimate CBT from radiation emitted by the tympanic membrane, assuming equilibrium with carotid arterial blood. Accuracy depends strongly on probe orientation, ear-canal anatomy, and the absence of airflow or obstruction {cite:p}`childs_tympanic_1999`. Tympanic canal temperatures can diverge from oesophageal and rectal readings during exercise and {ref}`recovery <label-recovery>`, particularly under convective airflow or incomplete sealing {cite:p}`gagnon_aural_2010`.

**Temporal-artery infrared and dual- or zero-heat-flux sensors.** Non-invasive infrared thermometers measure surface emission along the temporal artery, whereas dual-heat-flux (DHF) and zero-heat-flux (ZHF) sensors infer deep tissue temperature by combining paired heat-flux paths of different thermal resistances {cite:p}`kitamura_development_2010`. These techniques are useful for ambulatory or field monitoring but require stable ambient conditions and careful calibration; drift can occur with sweating or variable skin perfusion {cite:p}`brauer_accuracy_2020`.

**Pulmonary artery and bladder catheters.** Invasive thermistors located in the pulmonary artery directly measure mixed venous blood and remain the clinical *gold standard*. Bladder thermistors, integrated into urinary catheters, are less invasive and show small mean differences (≤ 0.2 °C) relative to pulmonary-artery temperature under stable hemodynamic conditions {cite:p}`lefrant_temperature_2003`. Their use is restricted to medical or highly controlled research applications.

### Agreeability across sensor types

Inter-site comparisons show consistent but physiologically interpretable biases that depend on tissue depth, perfusion, and the rate of thermal change. Under steady conditions, rectal, intestinal, and oesophageal temperatures differ by less than 0.3 °C, but diverge during dynamic heating or cooling. Oesophageal readings track central blood temperature most closely, typically within ±0.2 °C of pulmonary-artery measurements and with the fastest response (≤10 s) {cite:p}`lefrant_temperature_2003,robinson_oesophageal_1998`. Rectal temperature lags oesophageal by 10--25 min and averages 0.3--0.6 °C lower during heating phases {cite:p}`lee_core_2000`. Intestinal or telemetry-pill temperatures are intermediate, initially 0.4--0.9 °C lower than rectal when the capsule remains in the stomach, but converging to ≤ 0.2 °C once it passes into the small intestine {cite:p}`notley_time_2021,sparling_monitoring_1993,teunissen_telemetry_2012`.

Bladder thermistors agree well with pulmonary-artery temperature (bias ≤ 0.2 °C) under stable perfusion, but deviate during low urine flow or bypass {cite:p}`lefrant_temperature_2003`. Rectal and oesophageal values remain within 0.4 °C of pulmonary-artery readings at rest, confirming their reliability as integrated estimates of body heat content {cite:p}`robinson_oesophageal_1998`.

Non-invasive ear and forehead methods exhibit larger scatter. Infrared tympanic thermometers underestimate deep-core temperature by 0.3 °C at rest and by \~1 °C during exercise, with errors increasing to \>1.5 °C when rectal temperature exceeds 39 °C {cite:p}`craig_infrared_2002,huggins_comparison_2012`. Even meta-analytic evidence across \>30 studies indicates wide limits of agreement (± 1.3 °C), making tympanic readings unreliable for precise monitoring {cite:p}`craig_infrared_2002`. Temporal-artery infrared and axillary measures show similar variability {cite:p}`robinson_oesophageal_1998`.

Zero-heat-flux (ZHF) thermometers show markedly improved accuracy, differing from pulmonary-artery temperature by only --0.06 °C (mean) with limits of agreement ± 0.89 °C {cite:p}`verheyden_clinical_2022`. These perform comparably to oesophageal and bladder probes under anaesthesia when the forehead site remains perfused. Emerging dual-heat-flux and heart-rate-derived algorithms can estimate core temperature non-invasively with mean biases around 0.2--0.3 °C, but limits of agreement ± 0.6--0.7 °C {cite:p}`agostinelli_validity_2023,verdel_reliability_2021`. They remain sensitive to local perfusion and ambient conditions but hold promise for field and occupational studies.

Overall, oesophageal and pulmonary-artery sites provide the most responsive and accurate dynamic indicators of CBT, while rectal, intestinal, and bladder sensors yield stable integrated measures of stored heat. Infrared and heat-flux methods enable practical non-invasive alternatives when calibration and contextual metadata are explicitly documented.

### Known confounders and modifiers

Core body temperature is shaped by multiple intrinsic and extrinsic influences that must be measured, controlled, or statistically adjusted in experimental protocols. These factors act through metabolic, hormonal, neural, and environmental pathways that shift the {ref}`thermoregulatory set-point <label-thermoregulatory-set-point>` or modify heat-exchange efficiency.

**Circadian influences.** CBT exhibits a robust endogenous oscillation of approximately 0.8--1.0 °C under normal {ref}`entrainment <label-entrainment>`, generated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus and peripheral clock gene networks {cite:p}`krauchi_how_2002,krauchi_circadian_1994`. The {ref}`nadir <label-nadir>` occurs near 04:00--05:00 h, coinciding with peak melatonin and lowest alertness, while the {ref}`acrophase <label-acrophase>` appears around 16:00--18:00 h. This rhythm persists under constant posture and ambient conditions, confirming endogenous control, though masking by sleep, posture, and activity can shift observed CBT by ±0.3 °C {cite:p}`kelly_body_2006,krauchi_how_2002,refinetti_analysis_1992`. Ambient light exposure {cite:p}`dijk_effect_1991,elkounni_acute_2025` and recent travel may also alter {ref}`phase <label-phase>` and amplitude. Session timing, {ref}`chronotype <label-chronotype>` alignment, and relative phase (hours from habitual wake time or {ref}`dim-light melatonin onset <label-dim-light-melatonin-onset>`) should therefore be logged and, where possible, standardised across participants.

**Sex.** Generally, females exhibit higher core temperatures than males {cite:p}`diamond_one_2021,waalen_is_2011`. During exercise under heat stress, early observational studies suggested women experienced greater and more rapid increases in core temperature than men; however, these differences are largely explained by confounding factors such as a lower body mass, lower aerobic capacity, and a higher relative metabolic heat production in females {cite:p}`debray_sex-related_2025,dervis_comparison_2016,gagnon_does_2012`. When males and females are appropriately matched for physical characteristics and the biophysical requirement for heat loss, core temperature responses are generally similar, though women may experience slightly greater increases (0.2°C to 0.4°C) only during severe, uncompensable heat stress {cite:p}`lei_influence_2017`. This difference may be clinically insignificant {cite:p}`giersch_regulation_2025`.

**Sex hormones and reproductive status.** Progesterone elevates the hypothalamic {ref}`thermoregulatory set-point <label-thermoregulatory-set-point>`, increasing resting core body temperature by approximately 0.3--0.5 °C during the luteal phase. In contrast, oestrogen facilitates peripheral heat dissipation through cutaneous {ref}`vasodilation <label-vasodilation>` and enhanced skin perfusion {cite:p}`charkoudian_sex_2016`. Women using combined oral contraceptives exhibit a constant, mid-luteal-like CBT profile, typically 0.3--0.4 °C higher than naturally cycling follicular-phase values, with reduced circadian amplitude and phase stabilisation {cite:p}`baker_oral_2001,baker_circadian_2007,stephenson_thermoregulation_1993`. Accurate determination of menstrual or contraceptive phase is therefore essential in mixed-sex studies.

During perimenopause, fluctuating oestrogen levels destabilise hypothalamic feedback and narrow the {ref}`thermoneutral zone <label-thermoneutral-zone>`, producing transient hyperthermic flushes {cite:p}`freedman_menopausal_2014`. Oestrogen therapy alone lowers the vasodilation and sweating thresholds, effectively narrowing the {ref}`interthreshold zone <label-interthreshold-zone>` {cite:p}`freedman_reduced_1999,gupta_thermoregulation_2000`, whereas the addition of progestin raises the defended temperature and CBT by \~ 0.3 °C, mirroring premenopausal luteal physiology {cite:p}`baker_temperature_2020,stachenfeld_estrogen_2000`. Exogenous sex hormones used in contraception, menopausal hormone therapy, or gender-affirming treatment likewise alter thermoregulatory set-point and amplitude: oestradiol-dominant regimens generally lower or stabilise CBT via enhanced vasodilation, while progestin-dominant or androgenic formulations elevate CBT and attenuate peripheral heat loss {cite:p}`baker_temperature_2020,freedman_estrogen_2002,gombert-labedens_effects_2025`. Precise documentation of hormonal formulation, dosage, and treatment duration is essential for accurate cross-participant comparison.

**Pregnancy.** Pregnancy raises resting CBT by approximately 0.3--0.5 °C above pre-pregnancy values, driven by progesterone-mediated elevation of the thermoregulatory set-point, increased metabolic rate, and expanded blood volume {cite:p}`soma-pillay_physiological_2016`. Cardiac output rises by 30--50 % and resting HR by 10--20 beats·min⁻¹, substantially altering the cardiovascular thermoregulatory response {cite:p}`robson_serial_1989`. The thermoneutral zone narrows, and the core temperature threshold for sweating decreases in the third trimester as oestrogen-to-progesterone ratios shift {cite:p}`soma-pillay_physiological_2016`. Because pregnancy produces a persistent, hormonally driven shift in all thermoregulatory effector thresholds and baselines, pregnant participants require separate analysis; pooling with non-pregnant groups without stratification will confound any CBT-based outcome variable.

**Age.** Advancing age reduces sweat-gland output and {ref}`cutaneous vasodilation <label-vasodilation>`, slowing heat loss and increasing peak CBT during heat stress {cite:p}`kenney_invited_2003`. Under thermoneutral conditions, core temperature differences by age were found to be \~ 0.15 °C based on data from over 18.000 people {cite:p}`waalen_is_2011`.

**Fitness.** Aerobically trained individuals show slightly lower resting CBT (\~0.2 °C) and reduced heat-storage rates for a given metabolic load, owing to expanded plasma volume, earlier sweat onset, and efficient {ref}`peripheral perfusion <label-peripheral-circulation>` {cite:p}`periard_exercise_2021`.

**Heat {ref}`acclimation <label-acclimation>`.** Repeated daily exposures, for even as few as 5 days, lower resting CBT by \~0.2--0.4 °C {cite:p}`daanen_heat_2018,taylor_human_2014`. Acclimated individuals maintain smaller CBT excursions and faster recovery under equivalent heat loads {cite:p}`garrett_induction_2009`. Acclimation adaptations partially reverse within 2--3 weeks of heat withdrawal; however, re-acclimation occurs quicker than initial acclimation {cite:p}`daanen_heat_2018`.

**Cold acclimation.** Repeated exposures to cool or cold environments produce different adaptations depending on exposure pattern. Insulative cold acclimation (intermittent whole-body cooling) enhances peripheral vasoconstriction, reducing heat loss and stabilising CBT at a lower metabolic cost {cite:p}`castellani_human_2016`. Metabolic cold acclimation (prolonged mild cold) increases non-shivering thermogenesis, primarily through sympathetic activation of brown adipose tissue, raising resting metabolic rate and slightly elevating CBT {cite:p}`castellani_human_2016,stocks_human_2004`. Researchers conducting protocols that include cold exposure, or who recruit participants recently arrived from cold climates, should document recent thermal exposure history and not assume that acclimation effects are limited to heat.

**Hydration and nutrition.** Dehydration ≥ 2 % body mass elevates CBT by 0.2--0.5 °C for a given workload due to plasma-volume reduction, impaired {ref}`skin blood flow <label-cutaneous-blood-flow>`, and reduced sweat rate {cite:p}`sawka_hydration_2001`. Diet-induced {ref}`thermogenesis <label-thermogenesis>` (DIT) transiently raises CBT by \~0.2--0.4 °C, peaking 1--3 h after eating, depending on macronutrient content {cite:p}`westerterp_diet_2004`, while the presence of diabetes mellitus, insulin resistance, obesity or thyroid disease alters the impacts {cite:p}`tzeravini_diet_2024`. Caffeine and capsaicin stimulate metabolic heat production, whereas alcohol depresses {ref}`vasoconstriction <label-vasoconstriction>`, lowering CBT in cool conditions {cite:p}`michlig_effects_2016,romanovsky_thermoregulation_2007`.

**Body composition.** Greater subcutaneous adiposity increases insulation and {ref}`thermal inertia <label-thermal-inertia>`, lengthening CBT time constants by 20--30 % compared with lean individuals {cite:p}`havenith_individualized_2001`. Conversely, higher muscle mass and surface-to-mass ratio facilitate faster heat dissipation.

**Posture.** Posture changes (supine to upright) can transiently alter CBT by \~0.1--0.4 °C through redistribution of blood volume and convective exchange {cite:p}`donaldson_effect_1996,tikuisis_effect_1996`.

**Chronotype and sleep timing.** Interindividual differences in circadian phase shift CBT acrophase and amplitude {cite:p}`taillard_circadian_2003`. Evening types (*owls*) exhibit delayed peaks and elevated nocturnal troughs, while morning types (*larks*) peak earlier by around 2 hours {cite:p}`baehr_individual_2000,kerkhof_morning-type_1996`. Sleep deprivation may blunt CBT amplitude, delay phase onset and lower overall CBT the following day during wake hours {cite:p}`hibi_effect_2017`.

**Shift work and circadian disruption.** Chronic circadian misalignment — as experienced by rotating-shift and night workers — disrupts the normal 24-h CBT rhythm beyond the transient effects of individual poor-sleep nights. Night-shift workers show flattened CBT amplitudes (< 0.5 °C peak-to-nadir) and displaced acrophases that do not fully re-entrain to a daytime schedule even after rest days {cite:p}`sack_circadian_2007,arendt_shift_2010`. This persistent phase disruption is physiologically distinct from chronotype variation: it reflects desynchrony between the central pacemaker and peripheral clocks driven by conflicting zeitgebers (light, food timing, social cues). When shift workers participate in indoor thermal protocols, their CBT baseline and circadian phase may not align with clock time, making time-of-day matching across participants unreliable without concurrent melatonin or actigraphy-based phase assessment {cite:p}`sack_circadian_2007`.

**Neurodivergent** **populations.** Evidence from autism spectrum disorder (ASD), ADHD, and related neurodevelopmental conditions indicates altered autonomic balance and circadian regulation. Individuals with ASD frequently display attenuated CBT amplitude and increased day-to-day variability, suggesting weaker circadian entrainment {cite:p}`dellosso_biological_2022`. Melatonin secretion profiles in ASD are often delayed or reduced {cite:p}`wu_autism_2020`, producing phase lags of 1--4 h relative to typically developing controls, with corresponding CBT delays of similar magnitude {cite:p}`rossignol_melatonin_2011`. ADHD is associated with delayed sleep onset, longer sleep latency, and a consistent eveningness preference {cite:p}`snitselaar_sleep_2017`, all of which shift CBT acrophase later into the evening {cite:p}`bijlenga_body_2013`.

**Post-viral dysautonomia and long COVID.** Post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) can produce persistent hypothalamic and autonomic dysfunction, including temperature dysregulation, abnormal sweating, and intolerance of both heat and cold {cite:p}`davis_long_2023`. At the physiological level, disrupted sympathetic effector control shifts CBT baseline and thermoregulatory thresholds in ways that are not captured by standard medical history screens {cite:p}`dani_autonomic_2021`. Participants with a history of COVID-19 infection should be asked specifically about ongoing thermoregulatory symptoms; if present, these should be recorded as a participant-level variable and treated as a stratification factor.

**Underlying medical conditions and medications.** Fever and systemic inflammation raise the hypothalamic set-point via cytokine-mediated prostaglandin synthesis {cite:p}`romanovsky_thermoregulation_2007`. Thyroid dysfunctions, particularly hyperthyroidism, elevate basal metabolic rate and CBT, whereas hypothyroidism lowers it by a similar margin {cite:p}`iwen_effects_2018`. Autonomic neuropathy impairs {ref}`thermoeffector <label-thermoeffector>` control of skin blood flow and sweating, increasing vulnerability to heat and cold stress {cite:p}`cheshire_thermoregulatory_2016`. Diabetes mellitus, through autonomic and microvascular dysfunction, reduces heat-dissipation capacity and alters core temperature responses {cite:p}`kenny_body_2016,rutkove_impaired_2009`. Parkinson's patients have lower nighttime CBT following the severity of RSB symptoms {cite:p}`zhong_relationship_2013`. Spinal cord injury, especially lesions at or above T6, interrupts {ref}`sympathetic outflow <label-sympathetic-and-parasympathetic-outflow>` and sweating/{ref}`vasomotor control <label-vasomotor-control>`, leading to impaired {ref}`thermoregulation <label-thermoregulation>` {cite:p}`handrakis_thermodysregulation_2017,karlsson_overview_2006`. β-blockers, anticholinergics, sedatives, and SSRIs further modulate thermoregulatory control and should be screened {cite:p}`romanovsky_thermoregulation_2007`.

**Measurement {ref}`artefacts <label-artefact>`**. Shallow rectal insertion, oral breathing/cold or hot fluids, or cerumen/misalignment in tympanic readings bias results; for ingestible pills, ingestion/timing, GI motility and device bias matter {cite:p}`byrne_ingestible_2007,childs_tympanic_1999,hunt_systematic_2017`.

### Data handling methods

#### Sensor calibration

Sensor calibration is a critical step prior to data collection, particularly for ingestible telemetry pills and thermistors, to ensure measurement accuracy and correct for systematic bias. The standard approach involves evaluating each sensor individually against a certified, traceable reference thermometer in a circulated, heated water bath {cite:p}`byrne_ingestible_2007`. Calibration should be performed across a physiologically valid range of temperatures (typically three to five discrete points, such as 30 °C, 34 °C, 38 °C, and 42 °C). After allowing sufficient time for the sensor and water temperature to stabilise (e.g., \>4 minutes), simultaneous readings are recorded to establish a relationship {cite:p}`hunt_systematic_2017,lee_core_2000,obrien_telemetry_1998,simpson_traceability_2006`.

Because individual sensors can exhibit systematic biases or offsets from the manufacturer\'s stated accuracy, the water bath data is used to generate an individualised linear regression equation for each sensor. This individual equation is then applied to correct the raw data collected during the actual experiment. Alternatively, a generalised linear correction equation can be applied to the raw data, provided that a single-point preliminary check confirms the sensor falls within an acceptable initial error margin (e.g., ±0.5 °C of the reference thermometer) {cite:p}`hunt_systematic_2017`.

#### Data cleaning and correction

**{ref}`Filtering <label-filtering>` for {ref}`noise <label-noise>`.** Practical approaches include low-pass or moving-average {ref}`smoothing <label-smoothing>` (e.g., 30--60 s windows) to suppress motion/respiratory artefacts while retaining thermoregulatory trends {cite:p}`bongers_using_2015,byrne_ingestible_2007`. For ingestible capsules, brief telemetry gaps (\< \~2 min) can be linearly interpolated; longer gaps (often caused by the sensor going temporarily out of range of the receiver) are excluded or spline-interpolated with caution {cite:p}`bongers_using_2015`.

**Discarded data.** {ref}`Artefact <label-artefact>` removal should target non-physiological discontinuities. Specific instances requiring data exclusion include:
\- Data points recorded before a sensor is properly affixed or ingested, or during known periods of temporary removal (e.g., bathing).
\- Sudden, transient depressions in gastrointestinal or oesophageal temperature are clearly associated with the ingestion of cold fluids or food, as these reflect local cooling rather than true core body temperature changes. To avoid these data blocks, several protocols provide food/drinks at around 36.5--37 °C {cite:p}`byrne_ingestible_2007`.
\- Data from the early phase of ingestible capsule transit; some protocols systematically erase data from the first several hours (e.g., 6 hours {cite:p}`lee_core_2000`) post-ingestion to avoid the instability and systematic errors associated with stomach transit.

**Typical ranges.** Normal resting core body temperature is tightly maintained between 36.0 -- 37.5 °C {cite:p}`diamond_one_2021,mackowiak_critical_1992`. Valid physiological data during severe thermal stress typically stays within the limits of clinical hypothermia (\< 35.0 °C) and severe hyperthermia or heat stroke (\> 40.0--40.5 °C). Readings falling significantly outside of these physiological extremes (such as sensor drops below 30 °C in ambulatory humans) are generally indicative of technical problems, equipment failure, cold food/drink ingestion, or the sensor expelled from the body.

#### Derived parameters

From the filtered signal, several derived parameters may be computed:

- <u>{ref}`Baseline <label-baseline>` CBT (°C).</u> Mean temperature during the final 10--15 min before exposure.
- <u>ΔCBT (°C).</u> Difference between end- and start-exposure means; reflects net heat storage or dissipation.
- <u>Rate of rise (°C·h⁻¹).</u> Linear slope of CBT during exposure; indicates the rate of uncompensated heat storage {cite:p}`periard_exercise_2021`.
- <u>Peak CBT (°C).</u> Maximum observed temperature during the protocol.
- <u>Area under the curve (AUC, °C·min).</u> Integral of CBT above baseline across the session, often {ref}`normalised <label-normalization>` by duration (°C·min) to quantify total {ref}`thermal load <label-thermal-load>` {cite:p}`datta_quantification_2021`.
- <u>Recovery slope (°C·h⁻¹).</u> Rate of CBT decline post-exposure, used to assess heat-dissipation efficiency and vascular recovery kinetics.
